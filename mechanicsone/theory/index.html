Scope, and how to read this manual

This manual states, for every calculation the app performs, what relation is being evaluated, what principle it rests on, how the working form was reached from that principle, what it assumes, how it is solved, and where it stops being trustworthy. It is written to be consulted rather than read start to finish: each module is self-contained, and nothing later depends on anything earlier except the conventions fixed in the next two chapters.

It is not a textbook. It does not teach mechanics of materials from first principles, it does not set exercises, and it assumes a reader who has met the subject before and wants to know precisely what this particular program does with it. Where a derivation is given, it is given to fix the assumptions behind the app's own working relation, not to instruct in the subject.

Every module entry has the same shape, sections skipped where they do not apply rather than reordered:

Basis names the principle the relation rests on — an equilibrium statement, a compatibility condition, an energy theorem, a definition. Derivation shows how the app's working relation follows from that principle, including the steps where a choice was made, a substitution was convenient rather than necessary, or an expression was rewritten to survive floating-point arithmetic. Assumptions lists what must be true of the physical situation for the result to mean anything, each stated so its failure is recognisable rather than as a textbook caveat. Takes and Gives tabulate the symbols with their units and their meanings. Choices you have to make appears where a relation has more than one branch and the app shows every branch rather than picking one silently — a plate can fail in shear, in bearing, or by tearing, and the app is not going to guess which for you. What must always be true here lists the invariants and the monotone trends — every one of them is a property executed by the test suite over swept ranges of input, not a remark added to the prose afterward. Where it stops gives the limits and the singular points, and what the app does at each. Solution method says how the number is actually produced: closed form, a bracketed root search, a summation over segments. Limitations says what the result does not cover, including where a screen's scope is narrower than the underlying relation could support. Reading the result is about interpretation, and is where the mistakes this subject is known for are named plainly.

Every entry's heading carries a one-word badge, core or extended, and the two words mean something narrower than they sound. Core marks a relation that a first course in this subject treats as part of its own spine — the flexure formula, the torsion formula, Euler buckling — and extended marks one that such a course reaches only in a later chapter, an appendix or a design elective: curved beams, rotating discs, elastoplastic bending. The badge says nothing about how carefully a relation is implemented or verified; both kinds are held to the same reference values and the same identities, and both are equally likely to be the one a reader actually needs. It is there so that a reader scanning a chapter can tell which entries restate something they almost certainly already know and which introduce something they may not.

A note at the head of an entry saying it has no screen in this release means exactly that and nothing more: the relation is implemented, verified and documented here, but this version of the app provides no way to put a problem of that shape in front of it. The note gives the reason in each case.

The formulas, symbols, units, branching, invariants, boundaries and applicability guards printed in each entry are generated directly from the same specification file the application itself reads at launch. A relation printed here is therefore the relation the app evaluates, not a description of it that could quietly fall out of step. The surrounding prose — basis, derivation, method, limitations, reading — is written by hand and checked against the code: every module names the functions it describes, and the build refuses to run if one of those names no longer resolves to anything.

Notation, sign conventions and units

Most disagreements between two calculations that are each internally correct turn out to be disagreements about convention rather than about mechanics. This chapter fixes the ones this app uses, and says outright where this subject does not agree with itself.

Direct stress and strain. Tension positive, extension positive, throughout. A negative normal stress is compressive; nothing on this app's screens switches that around, unlike the sign convention some soil-mechanics and some concrete-design texts prefer.

Shear force and bending moment along a beam. Internally, one convention governs every beam calculation this app performs: a load is positive downward, a reaction is positive upward, an applied couple is positive clockwise, and a bending moment is positive when it sags the beam — tension on the underside. That single, fixed convention is what lets the identities tying the diagrams together (the moment is the integral of the shear, the shear the negative integral of the load) hold in one consistent form throughout the app rather than needing a sign correction chosen case by case. What is not fixed is how a reader sees it: two long-standing textbook traditions draw the shear diagram as mirror images of each other, and the screen that draws these diagrams offers that choice openly, mapping the same underlying, internally consistent numbers onto whichever drawn convention is selected. A diagram that looks upside down against a reader's memory of the subject is almost always this display choice, not an error in the numbers underneath it.

Angles. Radians internally, always; degrees are a display-edge convenience, converted on the way in and never carried past it. Rotations and moments follow the right-hand rule about the axis named, so a positive moment about an out-of-plane axis turns the first in-plane axis toward the second.

Section properties. Second moments of area are about centroidal axes unless a symbol says otherwise, and a hole removed from a section is subtracted as a negative area rather than typed in as one — the sign lives in how a piece is combined, not in a parameter a reader could get backwards. Where a section has no axis of symmetry, the app reports a full stress state rather than the single number a symmetric section would need, because collapsing the two would silently assume the section is not what the reader built.

Units: metres, newtons, pascals and radians inside; a choice of two practical systems on screen. Every kernel relation is evaluated in base SI — metres, newtons, pascals, radians — and every screen lets the reader work in either SI engineering units (millimetres, kilonewtons, megapascals) or US customary ones (inches, kips, ksi). The conversion happens once, at the boundary where a value crosses from the interface into the calculation or back, and nowhere else: nothing in the kernel, the composition layer or the solver carries any notion of which unit a reader prefers, and nothing converts a value twice or relabels it without converting it at all. Switching the displayed system therefore always changes the number shown, never merely the letters beside it — a field that kept showing the same digits under a different unit label would be reporting something false, and the boundary is built so that failure mode cannot occur by construction rather than by discipline.

Material and section data. Where the app carries reference numbers of its own — elastic constants, yield strengths, standard section geometry — the source is a public-domain government reference, not a textbook's own appendix table, and is named where it is used. A number attributed to no source on screen is one the app computed from what was typed in, not one it looked up.

Solution methods used throughout

Most of what this app computes is closed form: a relation with a known solution, evaluated directly from the inputs. Where that is not possible, one of a small number of numerical methods is used, described once here rather than separately in every module that depends on it.

Direct evaluation. The default, and the overwhelming majority of what the app does. Nothing iterates and nothing is approximated beyond the floating- point arithmetic itself. Where the textbook form of an expression is a difference of two nearly equal quantities at exactly the input a reader is likely to try — a bar whose two end widths approach each other, a Poisson's ratio approaching the incompressible limit, an impact factor at a vanishing drop height, a true strain at a small engineering strain — the app does not evaluate that form. It evaluates an algebraically equivalent one built around a logarithm-of-one-plus or a difference-quotient identity that has nothing to cancel, and returns the ordinary closed-form answer exactly at the point the textbook form would have divided zero by zero. The high-precision layer of verification exists partly to confirm that a rewritten expression still computes the same quantity as the one it replaced, at precision the rewrite itself could not check on its own.

Bracketed root-finding. Every inversion and every peak search in this app is bracketed, and there is no bare Newton iteration anywhere. The hardest case is the one that fixes the rule: an eccentrically loaded column's secant formula is the one place where the unknown load appears inside a secant of itself, no rearrangement removes it, and the relation has a pole at the column's own critical load — so an iteration starting from a guess can walk past that pole and return a number from the wrong branch, which is worse than a refusal because it looks like an answer. A bracket is taken from the physics instead: two loads known to sit either side of the answer, with the critical load as a hard, provable upper limit. The same discipline is applied wherever else a position or a depth has to be found rather than evaluated — a hollow shaft's diameter against an allowable stress, the neutral axis of a cracked reinforced-concrete section, the level that halves a section's area, the stations where a beam's shear force or its slope pass through zero — not because those relations are transcendental, most are not, but because a bracket taken from the geometry is what makes the answer deterministic: the method cannot converge to a different root, because the problem guarantees no other root lies inside the bracket. Inside the bracket the root is found by Brent's method, which accelerates on a well-behaved function and falls back to bisection whenever the fast step would leave the bracket or stop shrinking it — the fallback is what makes the bracket a guarantee rather than a hope.

Summation over segments. A stepped shaft, a bar of several prismatic pieces, a beam carrying several loads: none of these need a new relation, only the same one applied piece by piece and added. The addition is where mistakes live, not the relation, so it is performed by a summation ordered to keep its own arithmetic honest rather than by adding terms in the order a caller happened to list them — a short stiff segment next to a long soft one puts terms many orders of magnitude apart into the same total, and naive left-to-right addition can quietly lose the smaller one.

Singularity functions. A beam's shear force and bending moment, as a function of position, are built from Macaulay's bracket notation — a family of functions that switch on at the position of each load and stay on beyond it — rather than written as a separate polynomial on each span with matching conditions at every join. The two describe the same function; the bracket form is the one that a computer can evaluate at an arbitrary position without first working out which span that position falls in.

Small linear systems, solved without a linear-algebra library. A handful of modules reduce to a system of two or three linear equations — two redundant forces in a composite bar, three strain-gauge readings resolved into a full plane strain state — and these are solved by explicit elimination or Cramer's rule written out by hand, not by calling a general solver. The kernel this app shares with its two front ends imports nothing beyond the standard mathematics library, by design, so that the same arithmetic can be read and re-implemented line for line in either language without a matrix library existing on both sides of the boundary.

Guard values, not exceptions -- and refusals, which are neither. No function in the computational kernel raises an error for a bad input; it returns a value chosen for what it means, and lets that value propagate visibly to the screen rather than stopping the calculation. Separate from that, and deliberately kept separate, the layer that assembles those functions into whole sections and whole beams does refuse some inputs outright and says which one is the problem -- a description that removes more material than it adds is not a section, a beam with more supports than equilibrium can resolve is not a determinate beam, and a shear distribution cannot be taken through a rotated primitive. A refusal of that kind is a correct answer to a question that has none, and reporting it as a refusal rather than as a number is the point of distinguishing the two. A quantity with no physical reading at all — a negative area, an elastic modulus of zero used as a divisor, a Poisson's ratio outside the range any material can have — returns a signalling not-a-number. A quantity approaching a genuine physical limit rather than an invalid state — a column loaded at exactly its own critical load, a bulk modulus as a material approaches incompressibility — is allowed to grow toward the infinity that the mathematics actually produces there, because that is what the physics is doing, not a defect in the arithmetic. What every one of these choices has in common is that it is deliberate and stated once, here, rather than decided differently in each module: an invalid input and a limiting one are different things, and only one of them is a mistake.

How the results were verified

Results are checked in five ways, and the independence is the point of the arrangement: each layer is capable of catching a class of error that the other four cannot, so agreement across several of them means something that agreement with any one alone would not. The five do not each cover every module, and it would be misleading to say they did -- the printed-table and symbolic layers exist only where there is a printed table to check against or an implicit equation to prove a closed form solves, which is a minority of modules; the high-precision layer and the property tests cover every module. Where two layers happen to draw on the same published document, that overlap is recorded alongside the fixtures rather than counted twice: they are then independent of this app's arithmetic but not of each other.

Printed reference values. Worked examples and tabulated results, transcribed at the precision the source prints them — usually three or four significant figures. This layer is independent of nothing about the app's own arithmetic; it exists to catch a relation transcribed wrongly in the first place, or a quantity computed in the wrong unit, and it catches those reliably because it asks the simplest possible question: does the app reach the number the reference reaches.

High-precision recomputation. The same relations evaluated again from their defining equations, independently of the app's own code, using arbitrary-precision arithmetic — typically sixty significant figures, fewer where the calculation involves an integral expensive enough that sixty digits of it would not be affordable to generate. The generating scripts do not import this app's package, for the obvious reason that a reference value produced by the program under test would agree with it forever and prove nothing. This layer catches conditioning and cancellation: the cases where an algebraically correct expression, evaluated as printed rather than rewritten, would quietly lose its significant digits.

Symbolic proof. Wherever this app's working relation is a closed-form expression claimed to solve an equation that is normally left implicit — a neutral radius defined by an integral, a thick-walled cylinder's stress field defined by an equilibrium equation rather than by a formula — a symbolic algebra system proves that the closed form actually satisfies that equation, rather than merely producing numbers that look plausible at a handful of points. Each such proof is itself tested by deliberately corrupting the relation it is proving and confirming the proof then fails; a proof that cannot fail is not verifying anything.

Property tests. The invariants and the monotone trends named under each module, checked by an automated search over swept ranges of input rather than at a handful of chosen points. This is the layer that catches the error hiding between the points a person would have thought to try by hand — a sign that is wrong only when two particular inputs have the same sign, a branch that is chosen correctly everywhere except at the one boundary between two cases.

Independent public-domain sources. Values published in two US government engineering references — the Air Force Flight Dynamics Laboratory's Stress Analysis Manual and NASA's Astronautic Structures Manual — compared against this app's own results from the same inputs. This is the only layer independent of the textbooks the rest of the app was built from, and so the only one able to catch a misreading of them rather than merely a miscalculation from them.

On the reliability of published sources. This layer has, more than once, found the published value to be the one at fault rather than the app's. Chasing one such disagreement to the bottom found a worked example whose own printed steps contradict each other on the same page — a beam's bending calculation and its shear calculation use two different values for the same half-depth, and only one of them is consistent with the shape being analysed. Another turned out to carry a torsion constant printed exactly one power of the section's own side length short of the unit it is supposed to have, an error visible from the units alone without touching this app's result at all. A third had swapped which of two nearly equal radii belongs in which position of a fraction. Each of those is recorded and adjudicated in writing, on grounds that do not depend on this app's own implementation, rather than resolved by quietly adjusting the app until it matched the printed page. Where this manual states that a published figure is wrong, it is because a disagreement was chased to a cause outside this app and written down, not because the app disagreed and was assumed to be right.

Beyond the five: two languages, one specification. Every relation in this app is implemented twice, once for each of its two front ends, and two separate checks hold the pair together. The first is exhaustive and asks only about existence: every public name in each of the five layers of the computational core is required to be present on both sides, so a relation that exists in one language and simply does not exist in the other fails the build. That is the failure no fixture can catch, because a fixture nobody wrote compares nothing. The second asks about values: a shared body of reference cases is evaluated by both implementations and compared to the last bit either can represent. It covers the areas that reference set names rather than every relation one by one — so it is the first check that guarantees nothing is missing, and the second that guarantees the two agree wherever they are compared. Both implementations read the same specification file at launch and check that they were handed the same one by comparing its hash — so an update that changes what the app computes cannot reach only one of the two ends of it.

Foundations and Axially Loaded Members

M01   Axial Stress and Strain core

One force over one area. Every later screen reduces to this at the last step.

σ=P/A τ_avg=V/A ε=δ/L n=σ_fail/σ_allow

Basis

A prismatic bar carrying a force along its own axis is in equilibrium: cut it anywhere and the internal force at the cut must balance whatever is applied beyond the cut. Saint-Venant's principle is what lets that internal force be treated as a stress smeared evenly over the whole cross-section rather than concentrated near wherever the load is actually attached -- true away from the ends and away from any hole or step, and stated as a limitation below rather than assumed silently.

Derivation

Divide the internal force by the area it acts on and the result is the mean normal stress on that cut. There is no more derivation than that for the tension and compression case: it is the definition of stress applied to a free body with only one internal force to resolve. Strain follows the same pattern one level down -- a change in length divided by the length it happened over -- and neither definition knows yet whether the bar is elastic, so neither one is limited to it.

Shear across a fastener is the same division with a different area, and it is where the module earns its place rather than being a restatement of the axial case: a bolt or a pin can be cut by the joint on one plane or on two, depending on how the plates around it are arranged, and the two arrangements put the same load through either one cross-sectional area or two. The function takes which case applies as an explicit argument rather than asking a caller to halve the answer afterwards, because that halving is exactly the step a reader forgets.

Bearing stress is a third division again, and a different physical quantity from either of the other two even though it has the same units: it is not a stress transmitted through a section at all, but a contact pressure between a pin and the wall of the hole it sits in, spread -- by convention, not by an exact elasticity solution -- over the flat projected area of the hole rather than its curved surface. Factor of safety and required area are the same division read in the two directions a designer needs: how much margin an actual stress leaves, and how much area an allowable stress demands.

τ=V/A (single shear) τ=V/(2A) (double shear)
σ_b=P/A_b

Assumptions

  • The force acts along the centroidal axis of a prismatic bar, so the stress is uniform over the section rather than varying across it.
  • The point of interest is far enough from the load's point of application, and from any hole or change of section, that Saint-Venant's principle has already smoothed out the local disturbance.
  • Bearing stress is a nominal, code-style number -- load over projected contact area -- not the peak contact pressure an elasticity solution for a pin in a hole would give.

What must always be true here. Each of these is a test in the suite, not a remark:

  • The stress multiplied back by the area returns the applied load exactly
  • Sizing an area for an allowable stress, then reading the stress that area carries, returns the allowable stress
  • sigma decreases monotonically as A increases at fixed P

Where it stops.

  • A -> 0 — sigma diverges

Solution method

Direct evaluation throughout; nothing here iterates. Every quantity is one division, guarded so that a non-positive area returns a signalling not-a-number rather than the infinity the arithmetic alone would produce -- the two read the same to a human glancing at a screen, but only one of them is a value a later calculation can be trusted to propagate correctly. Factor of safety treats a working stress of exactly zero as an infinite margin rather than a division failure, since a member carrying no stress at all is, trivially, safe against any failure stress.

Limitations

  • This module reports the internal force and area at a single cut, already resolved; it does not itself determine what that internal force is from a set of applied loads -- that is either read directly from a simple loading or supplied by the beam and frame modules elsewhere in the app.
  • Bearing stress is the simple load-over-projected-area convention used for sizing; it is not a substitute for the actual contact-mechanics stress distribution around a loaded hole.
  • Nothing here accounts for a stress concentration at a hole, fillet or shoulder -- that is a separate module, and the nominal stress computed here is exactly the quantity a concentration factor from that module would multiply.

Reading the result

The single-versus-double shear choice is the one place this simple a module still catches people, because the two answers differ by a factor of two and both look equally plausible on the screen. Double shear applies whenever the member being checked is sandwiched so that the load has two planes to cross -- a bolt through the middle plate of a three-plate lap joint is the standard picture -- and single shear whenever it has only one. The bearing stress reported alongside is a different quantity again, checking a different failure mode (crushing the hole wall) from either shear case (cutting the fastener), and a joint is only as strong as whichever of the modes its weakest dimension governs.

M02   Elastic Constants core

Any two of E, G, K and nu fix the other two. Six inversion paths that must all agree.

G=E/(2(1+ν)) K=E/(3(1-2ν)) e_v=((1-2ν)/E)(σ_x+σ_y+σ_z)

Basis

An isotropic linear-elastic material has exactly two independent elastic constants, not four. Young's modulus, the shear modulus, the bulk modulus and Poisson's ratio are four names in common use, but any two of them fix the other two, because all four are different combinations of the same underlying pair (equivalently, the two Lame parameters). The module exists to carry that fact through arithmetic rather than to state it: whichever two a reader has to hand, the other two follow, and asking the same question a second way -- ten more different two-constant starting points, in principle -- has to land back on the same third and fourth constant every time.

Derivation

The shear modulus and bulk modulus each follow from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio by the standard isotropic relations, and the reverse directions -- Poisson's ratio from Young's modulus and the shear modulus, Young's modulus from the bulk and shear moduli -- invert those same two relations rather than approximating them. Because six ordered pairs can be chosen from four constants, this module exposes six such paths rather than one, and the closing identity that matters is not any single formula but that every path which should reach the same third constant does: compute the shear modulus from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio, then recover Poisson's ratio from Young's modulus and that shear modulus, and the original input must come back unchanged.

The bulk modulus is where this module's real content lives, because its formula divides by 1 - 2*nu and that quantity is small exactly at the input a reader is most likely to try: an incompressible or nearly incompressible material, such as rubber near nu = 0.5. The division itself is not the problem -- a bulk modulus that grows without bound as a material becomes incompressible is the correct physical answer, since an incompressible material needs infinite pressure to change its volume by any amount at all. The problem is arithmetic: recovering 1 - 2*nu by subtracting 2*nu from one, when nu is itself a value like 0.4999 entered by a reader, throws away digits before the division that uses it even begins. The dilatation (fractional volume change under a general three-axis stress state) carries the same factor for the same reason, since it is built from the same compressibility term multiplying the sum of the three normal stresses.

λ=Eν/((1+ν)(1-2ν))
e_v=(1-2ν) (σ_x+σ_y+σ_z)/E

Assumptions

  • The material is isotropic: its elastic response is the same in every direction, so a single pair of constants describes it completely.
  • The material is linear-elastic over the stress range in question, so the constants are genuinely constants rather than local slopes of a curved response.
  • Poisson's ratio lies strictly between -1 and 0.5; a value at either bound corresponds to a material with no finite shear or bulk stiffness respectively, not to a numerically awkward but otherwise ordinary material.

What must always be true here. Each of these is a test in the suite, not a remark:

  • All six two-constant inversion paths return the same third constant
  • G computed from (E,nu) then nu recovered from (E,G) returns the original nu
  • K increases without bound as nu approaches 0.5

Where it stops.

  • nu -> 0.5 — K diverges; the material becomes incompressible
  • nu -> -1 — G diverges

Solution method

Closed form throughout, but with one quantity treated as a first-class citizen rather than recomputed at every step: wherever a caller already has 1 - 2*nu to hand -- most usefully, computed once at higher precision than the nu it came from -- the bulk-modulus relation accepts it directly instead of subtracting it back out of a rounded Poisson's ratio a second time. The convenience form that takes nu alone still exists and pays the digits that subtraction costs; a warning appears above nu = 0.49, chosen so that the compressibility term still carries roughly eleven significant digits at that threshold and has visibly fewer beyond it.

Limitations

  • This module reports the constants of an assumed-isotropic material; it has no way to detect or represent a material whose stiffness genuinely depends on direction.
  • The six inversion paths cover every ordered pair drawn from Young's modulus, the shear modulus, the bulk modulus and Poisson's ratio; a reader who instead has one of the two Lame parameters must still supply Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio to reach it, because that parameter is not one of the four this screen accepts as an input.

Reading the result

The identity worth checking by hand, because it is also the fastest way to catch a mistyped constant, is that all six routes through this module from any two starting constants land on the same other two -- there is no route that is more authoritative than another, so a discrepancy always means an input, not a formula, is wrong. A bulk modulus that reads as very large, or a warning about the incompressible limit, is telling a reader their Poisson's ratio is close to one half; it is not telling them anything is broken.

Warning. Approaching incompressibility: the bulk modulus is ill-conditioned and volumetric results lose precision.

Every other relation in this manual takes a handful of numbers a reader can type or drag; this one takes a whole measured curve -- a file of stress and strain readings off a testing machine -- so the app would first need a way to read a reader's own data file, and reading files is the one input route this release does not have. That route is held back to a later version together with the rest of the file-handling work, and a screen that could only reduce curves the app had invented for itself would be worse than no screen at all. The relation, its kernel and its verification are complete and held to the same standard as everything else here, which is why it is written up in full rather than left out: what is missing is the way in, not the mechanics.
σₜᵣᵤₑ=σ(1+ε) εₜᵣᵤₑ= ln (1+ε) Uᵣ=σₚₗ²/(2E)

Basis

A tensile test produces a list of stress and strain readings, not a formula, and every quantity a designer eventually quotes from it -- a yield strength, a modulus, a measure of ductility -- is a specific way of reading that list rather than a fresh law of mechanics. This module is unlike every other one in this chapter in that its input is data rather than dimensions, and its answers can genuinely depend on how densely the test was sampled; that dependence is reported rather than hidden.

Derivation

Most of the quantities here are direct readings: the peak stress reached is the ultimate stress; the percentage change in gauge length or in cross- sectional area between the start and the end of the test are the elongation and reduction-of-area ductility measures; the modulus of resilience is the area under the straight part of the curve up to the proportional limit, which for a linear response reduces to the closed form sigma_pl^2 / (2E); the modulus of toughness is the area under the whole curve, found by the trapezium rule over the measured points because the curve between two readings is not otherwise known, and inventing a smoother shape for it would report a precision the test itself does not have.

Two quantities need more than a reading. The offset yield strength -- the stress at which a specified permanent set, conventionally 0.2 per cent, has accumulated -- is found by walking the recorded points for the interval where the curve first crosses a line of slope E offset along the strain axis by that amount, then interpolating linearly inside that interval, rather than by any closed form: there is no continuous function to search for a root of, only a table of measurements, and treating it as continuous would again overstate the test's own precision. The two elastic-modulus readings serve different purposes and are computed differently for that reason: the secant modulus is stress over strain at one point, useful anywhere on the curve including well past yield, while the initial Young's modulus is fitted by least squares, through the origin and restricted to the lower part of the curve, to the straight portion alone -- fitting through the knee would drag the reported stiffness down, and fitting with a free intercept would let the line absorb whatever small seating error the test rig itself introduced rather than reporting the material's own stiffness.

True stress and true strain restate the same test against the instantaneous area and instantaneous gauge length rather than the original ones. True strain in particular is not an approximation of engineering strain but its exact logarithmic counterpart, and the two must agree closely for the small strains most structural analysis actually uses.

σₜᵣᵤₑ=σ(1+ε) εₜᵣᵤₑ= ln (1+ε)
Uₜ=∫ σ dε (trapezium rule over the measured points)

Assumptions

  • The supplied stress and strain arrays are paired, ordered readings from a single monotonically increasing test, not an arbitrary unordered set of points.
  • The offset yield strength depends on the offset chosen -- conventionally 0.2 per cent -- and different offsets give different, individually valid answers for the same curve.
  • The initial modulus is fitted from the lower part of the curve only; a curve with no clear straight portion, or too few points on it, will not yield a trustworthy fit even though the arithmetic still returns a number.

Solution method

The offset yield strength is not "the" yield strength in any absolute sense either; it is the strength at a chosen permanent-set convention, and changing the convention changes the number without either number being wrong.

M04   Stresses on an Inclined Section core

A bar pulled along one axis still carries shear on every other plane. The bridge to the transformation screens.

σ_θ=σ_x cos²θ τ_θ=-(1/2)σ_x sin 2θ

Basis

A bar loaded along its own axis is only simple to describe on the cross- section perpendicular to that axis. Cut it on any other plane and the same internal force has to be resolved into components normal and tangential to the new, inclined cut, and both components now carry stress -- including a shear stress on a member that, cut the obvious way, appeared to carry none at all.

Derivation

This is not a separate physical law from the general two-dimensional stress transformation used elsewhere in this app; it is that same transformation with the second normal stress and the shear stress on the reference face both set to zero, because a bar loaded only along its axis has nothing else to enter. The app computes it exactly that way -- by calling the general transformation kernel with those two terms zero -- rather than maintaining a second, superficially simpler formula that could drift out of step with the first. Writing the general result out for this special case gives a normal stress that varies as the cosine squared of the cut angle and a shear stress that varies as the sine of twice that angle, each scaled by the one nonzero input stress.

σ_θ=σ_x cos²θ
τ_θ=-(1/2)σ_x sin 2θ

M05   Strain Energy, Sudden and Impact Loading core

The same weight applied three ways gives three different stresses. Which way is a choice the user must make.

U=P²L/(2AE) nᵢₘₚ=1+√(1+2h/δₛₜ)

Basis

Work done on an elastic bar is stored, not lost, and for a linear-elastic member that stored energy is exactly the area under its own load-deflection line -- a triangle, so one half of load times deflection, which is the same statement as one half of stress times strain per unit volume. That single fact is enough to compare three different ways of applying the same final load, because energy conservation, not a separate formula for each case, is what connects them.

Derivation

For a load increased gradually from zero, the stored energy is the usual triangular area, and this is the baseline case: no amplification at all.

For the same load lowered onto the bar already at full value and then released -- applied suddenly rather than gradually -- the external work done as the bar stretches to its final deflection is the full load times that whole deflection, a rectangle rather than a triangle, twice the area of the gradual case for the same final deflection. Since the stored energy is still one half times stiffness times deflection squared regardless of how the load arrived, equating twice the gradual work to the same stored-energy formula forces the deflection under a suddenly applied load to be exactly twice the gradual, static deflection -- and because stress and deflection are both proportional to the same linear response, the stress doubles along with it. That factor of two is :func:sudden_load_factor, and it is a constant, not a computation: it follows from the ratio of a rectangle to a triangle of the same base and height, not from anything particular to the bar.

For a weight dropped from some height above the bar and caught by it, the same energy balance is extended one step further: the falling weight arrives with kinetic energy from the drop in addition to the work its own weight does over the final static deflection, and setting the sum of both equal to the stored elastic energy at the (now larger) peak deflection gives a quadratic in the deflection whose physically meaningful root is the closed form 1 + sqrt(1 + 2h/delta_static). Setting the drop height to zero in that closed form must recover the suddenly applied factor of exactly two, and does: the two derivations are two special cases of the same energy balance, and that limiting agreement is one of this module's own checked identities, not a coincidence stated after the fact.

U=P² L/(2AE) u=σ²/(2E)
nᵢₘₚ=1+√(1+2h/δₛₜ)

Assumptions

  • The bar remains linear-elastic throughout, including at the peak of the impact deflection; none of this module's results hold once any part of the load history takes the material past its proportional limit.
  • The impacting weight and the bar are treated as an ideal rigid mass meeting an ideal elastic spring: no energy is lost to local contact deformation, sound, heat or a supporting structure that is not perfectly rigid.
  • The static deflection supplied is the deflection the same final load would cause if applied gradually, computed the ordinary way, not a separate measured quantity.

  • h_drop -> 0 — sqrt(1+2h/d) - 1 cancels

Solution method

Closed form throughout, but the impact factor is evaluated as 1 plus a separately computed excess term rather than as the textbook expression written directly, because the interesting behaviour near a small drop height is in that excess and the textbook form loses it. sqrt(1 + x) - 1 cancels catastrophically as x (twice the drop height over the static deflection) approaches zero -- at x values small enough to matter, the naive subtraction can lose most or all of its significant digits, worse the smaller x gets. Rewritten as x / (sqrt(1+x) + 1), nothing is subtracted and nothing cancels, and the excess comes back accurate to full precision at any drop height, including zero. The peak deflection and peak stress under impact are then built from twice the static value plus that same excess term, with the factor of two written explicitly rather than recovered by subtracting one from a factor of two computed some other way -- getting that leading constant wrong is invisible at a large drop height and wrong by a factor of two at a zero drop, which is the case most likely to be tried first.

Limitations

  • Nothing here models the contact mechanics of the impact itself -- how the peak force is actually delivered over time, or losses to local indentation -- only the elastic energy balance before and after.
  • The three loading cases are alternatives for the same bar, not three loads applied in sequence; combining a sudden load with a subsequent drop is outside this module.

Reading the result

The single fact worth remembering about this module is the ordering: a gradually applied load produces the least stress, an suddenly applied one exactly double that, and a dropped weight more still, growing without bound as the drop height grows -- so the everyday habit of estimating an impact load by simply doubling the static case is only exactly right at zero drop height, and increasingly unconservative as the height grows from there. The screen shows all three side by side for exactly this reason: the point is the comparison, not any one of the three numbers alone.

M06   Elongation of Axially Loaded Members core

Four shapes of the same integral. Each degenerates into the prismatic case, and the degeneracy is where the arithmetic breaks.

δ=PL/(AE) δ_cone=4PL/(π E d_1 d_2) δ_wedge=PL ln (b_2/b_1)/(E t (b_2-b_1)) δ_self=γ L²/(2E)

Basis

The stretch of a prismatic bar under a constant axial force is the integral of strain over its length, and for a linear-elastic bar of constant section that integral collapses to the familiar PL/(AE). Every other case this module covers -- a bar that tapers, a bar hanging under its own weight, a bar built of several prismatic pieces end to end -- is the same integral carried out for a section, or a force, or both, that vary along the length, and each one is built to fall back onto the constant-section result the moment its own variation vanishes.

Derivation

A circular bar tapering linearly between two end diameters has an area that varies as the square of a linear function of position; carrying out integral of P dx / (A(x) E) for that area gives a closed form built from the product of the two end diameters, with no subtraction anywhere in it. A flat bar of constant thickness tapering linearly in width instead gives an area linear in position, and that integral produces a logarithm of the ratio of the two end widths -- which is exactly zero, divided by exactly zero, the moment the two widths are equal, since a prismatic bar is the untapered special case of the same integral.

A bar hanging under nothing but its own weight carries a force that varies linearly with position -- greatest at the point of support, zero at the free end -- rather than a constant force in a constant section, and integrating that varying internal force over the length in place of a constant one produces a stretch proportional to the square of the length rather than linear in it, with the material's own weight density standing in for an externally applied load.

A bar built of several prismatic segments end to end needs no new formula at all: each segment stretches by its own PL/(AE), using whatever force and section that particular segment carries, and the total stretch is the sum of the segments' individual stretches. The composition layer is what assembles that sum; the underlying relation is unchanged from the single-segment case.

δ_cone=4PL/(π E d_1 d_2)
δ_wedge=PL ln (b_2/b_1)/(E t (b_2-b_1))
δ_self=γ L²/(2E)

Assumptions

  • Every case here is linear-elastic and the taper, where present, is linear in the dimension that varies -- a diameter or a width that changes at a constant rate along the length.
  • The self-weight case assumes the bar hangs under its own weight alone, supported at one end, with no other axial load superposed on it (superposition applies if there is one).
  • A stepped bar's segments are each individually prismatic; a continuously varying section that is not one of the two closed-form taper shapes is outside this module.

  • The self-weight case is for a single prismatic bar hanging under its own weight; a stepped bar's self-weight contribution is not folded into the segment summation here.

M07   Statically Indeterminate Axial Members core

Equilibrium alone is not enough; compatibility supplies the missing equation.

δ₁=δ₂ and P₁+P₂=P ⟹ σ₁/E₁=σ₂/E₂, σ₁A₁+σ₂A₂=P

Basis

Two members that are constrained to stretch by exactly the same amount -- because they are fastened together at both ends, or because one is a rod running through the axis of a tube of a different material -- cannot each be found from equilibrium alone. Equilibrium supplies one equation (the two member forces sum to the applied load); compatibility supplies the second (the two members share a single, common elongation), and it is compatibility that turns an otherwise indeterminate problem into a determinate one.

Derivation

Writing each member's stretch as its own force times its own length over its own area and modulus, and setting the two stretches equal, gives a proportion between the two forces that depends only on each member's axial stiffness -- area times modulus over length. Combined with the equilibrium condition that the two forces sum to the applied load, that proportion gives each member's share of the load directly, in closed form, as that member's own stiffness over the combined stiffness of both: the stiffer member simply takes the larger fraction of the load, in exact proportion to how much stiffer it is.

This is a different combination from two members joined end to end (chained in series, covered by the equivalent stiffness elsewhere in this chapter, where flexibilities add) -- here the members act side by side and share one displacement, so stiffnesses add instead. The distinction is not a fine point: for one worked configuration the two combination rules differ by a factor of six and a half on the same numbers, and this module's own history includes a period where the specification pointed at the wrong one of the two before an interface had to actually display a number and the disagreement became visible. The self-check that tells the two apart is to verify, for whichever combined stiffness is used, that the whole assembly shortens by the applied load over that stiffness, and that each member's own share divided by its own stiffness reproduces that same shortening -- only the side-by-side, stiffness-adding combination satisfies that for both members simultaneously.

Stress in either member follows immediately once its share of the load is known, divided by that member's own area; the module computes it directly from the stiffness ratio rather than from the load share divided by the area a second time, so that a small area appears once in the calculation rather than twice.

δ₁=δ₂, P₁+P₂=P
P₁/P=A₁E₁/(A₁E₁+A₂E₂)

Assumptions

  • Both members are linear-elastic, prismatic, and constrained to undergo exactly the same axial elongation over the same length.
  • The applied load is shared between exactly two members; more than two sharing a common displacement is outside this module's closed form, though the same compatibility idea extends to it.

Reading the result

The physically intuitive check beside it is that the stiffer of the two members -- larger area, larger modulus, or both -- always takes the larger share, never the smaller: a rigid core surrounded by a soft sleeve carries nearly all of the load, not nearly none of it.

M08   Thermal Stress and Misfit core

A bar that cannot expand carries stress with no load on it at all.

δ_T=α Δ T L σ_restrained=-E α Δ T σ_gap=-E(αΔ T L-g)/L

Basis

A temperature change produces a strain in an unrestrained bar with no stress attached to it at all -- the bar simply gets longer or shorter, freely, and carries no internal force while it does. Stress appears only once something stops that free change from happening, and how much stress appears depends entirely on how completely the change is stopped.

Derivation

An unrestrained bar's thermal strain is the material's coefficient of expansion times the temperature change, by definition, and its elongation is that strain times the original length -- carrying no load, so this much needs no elasticity at all.

A bar fully restrained at both ends cannot elongate; whatever length change the temperature would otherwise have produced must instead appear as compressive or tensile strain held in by the restraint, and by Hooke's law that strain corresponds to a stress of -E times the coefficient of expansion times the temperature change -- carrying the load a real restraint would have to supply, but with no external force having been applied at all.

A bar restrained only after crossing a clearance gap combines both pictures in sequence: the bar is free until its unrestrained thermal elongation would exceed the gap, at which point it touches its restraint and only the excess beyond the gap is prevented, so the fully restrained formula applies to that excess elongation rather than to the whole of it. Setting the gap to zero must recover the fully restrained case exactly, and does, because a zero gap is the fully restrained case with no free travel subtracted from it first.

ε_T=α Δ T σ_restrained=-E α Δ T
σ_gap=-E(αΔ T L-g)/L once the gap has closed

Assumptions

  • The bar is prismatic, linear-elastic, and the temperature change is applied uniformly along its whole length; a temperature gradient along the bar is outside this module.
  • The restraint, where present, is rigid: it permits no elastic give of its own once contact is made, so all of the excess elongation beyond the gap is taken up as strain in the bar itself.
  • The gap, if any, is a fixed geometric clearance decided before the temperature changes, not itself a function of temperature.

M09   Stress Concentration Factors core

A geometric discontinuity multiplies the nominal stress. Two shapes have exact closed forms; the chart families do not, and are not guessed at.

σ_θ(a,θ)=σ(1-2 cos 2θ) K_t=3 K_t=1+2a/b=1+2√(a/ρ)

Basis

A hole in an otherwise uniformly stressed plate disturbs the stress field around it, and for two particular hole shapes under uniaxial tension that disturbed field has an exact closed-form solution rather than needing a numerical or experimental answer: a circular hole (Kirsch's solution) and, more generally, an elliptical one of any aspect ratio (Inglis's extension of it), of which the circle is the special case where the two axes are equal.

Derivation

Kirsch's solution gives the complete stress field around a circular hole in a wide plate under uniaxial tension; evaluated on the edge of the hole itself it collapses to a function of the single angle measured from the loading direction. That edge function is the primitive this module actually implements, rather than the peak concentration factor of three being written down as a constant on its own: reading the function's maximum, which occurs across the load direction, is what produces the familiar factor of three, and reading it along the load direction instead produces minus one -- a compressive stress on the same hole edge, in a plate that is nominally all tension, which is why a hole in a tension field can start a crack running perpendicular to what intuition would suggest. Deriving the factor of three from the field rather than stating it separately means the two numbers can never disagree with each other, because they are one function read at two angles.

The elliptical case (Inglis) generalises the same picture to a hole stretched either across or along the load, with a peak factor at the ends of the axis across the load equal to one plus twice the ratio of that across-axis semi-length to the along-axis one. An equal-length special case of that ratio is exactly one, giving back Kirsch's factor of three, so the elliptical and circular results meet exactly where the ellipse becomes a circle. The same factor can equivalently be written using the radius of curvature at the tip of the ellipse instead of the two semi-axis lengths directly -- a natural substitution because a very elongated ellipse behaves, near its tip, like a notch with that tip radius -- and the module offers both forms because different problems supply different starting information, checking that the two forms of the same factor agree being one of its own tests.

σ_θ(a,θ)=σ (1-2 cos 2θ)
Kₜ=1+2a/b=1+2√(a/ρ)

Assumptions

  • The plate is wide compared with the hole, so the hole sits in what is effectively an infinite uniaxial stress field rather than interacting with a nearby edge.
  • The material remains linear-elastic even at the peak stress the concentration factor predicts; the factor itself says nothing about what happens once that peak exceeds yield.
  • The elliptical form assumes the hole's axes are aligned with and perpendicular to the load direction; a hole at some other orientation is outside this module.

Solution method

Closed form throughout, evaluated directly with no iteration. The peak-stress function refuses a concentration factor below one, because a factor that reduces the nominal stress is not a stress concentration and the ordinary way to produce one by mistake is to enter the two elliptical semi-axes in the wrong order -- swapping which one is measured across the load and which along it turns a stress raiser into an apparent stress reliever, and the guard catches that rather than reporting the swapped answer as though it were legitimate.

Limitations

  • Only two hole geometries are covered: circular and elliptical, both under uniaxial tension in an otherwise uniform field. Filleted shoulders, U-notches and keyways -- the chart-based families a course in this subject usually covers alongside the two exact cases -- are deliberately not implemented, because the only public-domain chart data available for them is not usable as a source: its curve families share one legend with no values distinguishing which curve is which, so reading the chart correctly still leaves the governing parameter unknown, and no such factor is guessed at or interpolated from an unlabelled figure.
  • As with any linear-elastic stress concentration factor, the result stops being physically meaningful once the peak stress it predicts exceeds the material's yield strength; this module gives no post-yield correction.
  • The elliptical solution's limit as the hole degenerates toward a slit (the axis along the load shrinking toward zero) is a growing, unbounded factor consistent with a crack tip, which is a genuine result of the elasticity solution and not a numerical failure -- but it is also where linear elasticity itself stops being a useful physical model, and fracture mechanics proper is outside this app.

Reading the result

The comparison worth making on this screen is between the two hole shapes at equal area rather than at equal size in one direction: a hole elongated across the load direction concentrates stress far more than a round hole of the same area, while one elongated along the load concentrates it less -- which is exactly why a slot is normally oriented running along a loaded member rather than across it, and why the same elliptical hole rotated ninety degrees is a materially different design decision rather than a cosmetic one.

M10   Torsion of Circular Shafts core

Shear grows linearly from the axis. Everything else on this screen follows from that one fact.

τ=Tρ/J J_solid=π c⁴/2 J_tube=(π/2)(c_o⁴-c_i⁴) φ=TL/(JG)

Basis

A circular shaft is the one cross-section for which the assumption behind elementary torsion theory -- that a plane section stays plane and merely rotates rigidly about the shaft's axis -- is exactly true rather than approximately so, because the circular symmetry of the section matches the circular symmetry of the twist. Every other shape in this chapter warps out of its own plane under torque; the circle alone does not, and that is the entire reason its shear stress is linear in radius while every other shape needs either a series or an approximation.

Derivation

Because a radius stays straight and merely rotates, the shear strain at any point is directly proportional to its distance from the axis -- twice as far out, twice the strain -- and for a linear-elastic material the shear stress follows the same proportion. Requiring that the moment of this stress distribution about the axis equal the applied torque fixes the constant of proportionality: the stress at any radius is the torque times that radius, divided by the polar second moment of area, which is the same integral that turns the assumed linear distribution back into a moment. The angle of twist per unit length follows from the same shear-strain proportionality read the other way, integrated along the shaft's length under a constant torque.

A hollow shaft needs no new derivation, only the same integral carried out over an annulus instead of a full disc, which is why its polar second moment is the solid value for the outer radius less the solid value for the bore -- one subtraction, and the two closed forms share the same underlying function.

J_solid=π c⁴/2 J_tube=(π/2)(c_o⁴-c_i⁴)
φ=TL/(JG)

Assumptions

  • The shaft is prismatic and circular (solid or a concentric hollow circle), and the material is linear-elastic and isotropic.
  • Plane sections remain plane -- true for a circular section under pure torsion, unlike every noncircular section in this chapter.
  • The torque is applied at the ends (or, for a stress read at an interior station, taken as the internal torque already resolved there); the result is a statement about a cross-section away from any abrupt change in load or geometry.

As the bore approaches the outer radius, the same shaft is approaching the thin tube treated elsewhere in this chapter, and the two results have to meet in that limit -- a solid check that the general shaft formula and the thin-wall approximation are not two disconnected ideas but one physical picture seen two ways.

M11   Power Transmission and Shaft Sizing core

Rated power and speed give the torque; the allowable shear gives the diameter.

P_w=Tω=2π f T d=(16T/(πτ_allow))^(1/3)

Basis

Power is the rate of doing work, and for a rotating shaft that rate is torque times angular speed. A shaft delivering a specified power at a specified speed is therefore carrying a torque fixed by that one relation, and once the torque is known, sizing the shaft is an ordinary allowable-stress design problem read backwards through the circular-shaft formula elsewhere in this chapter.

Derivation

Power equals torque times angular speed, and angular speed is two pi times the rotational frequency, so the torque a shaft carries is the power divided by two pi times the frequency -- a direct rearrangement, not a separate physical law from the power-torque relation itself.

Sizing a solid shaft against an allowable shear stress inverts the ordinary circular-shaft stress formula: since the maximum stress on a solid shaft is sixteen times the torque over pi times the diameter cubed, solving that relation for the diameter gives a required diameter as the cube root of sixteen torque over pi times the allowable stress. A hollow shaft with a fixed ratio between its bore and its outer diameter needs the same inversion, but the polar section modulus now carries a factor that depends on that fixed ratio rather than being an unknown cubed on its own; the result is the same cube-root form multiplied by a correction that reduces to one, and to the solid-shaft diameter, exactly when the bore ratio is zero.

T=P_w/ω=P_w/(2π f)
d=(16T/(πτ_allow))^(1/3)

Assumptions

  • The power and speed are steady values, not instantaneous or fluctuating ones; a shaft under a pulsating torque needs a fatigue-aware allowable stress this module does not itself supply.
  • The allowable stress already reflects whatever safety factor and any stress-concentration allowance a designer intends; this module applies it exactly as given.
  • The hollow-shaft case assumes the ratio of bore to outer diameter is fixed in advance; if instead an absolute bore dimension is fixed, the sizing problem is a different one this module does not solve directly.

M12   Nonuniform and Indeterminate Torsion core

Shafts in series share the torque; shafts in parallel share the angle.

φ=Σᵢ Tᵢ Lᵢ/(Jᵢ Gᵢ) series: T common parallel: φ common

Basis

Three distinct situations share this module because they share one method: none of them needs a new relation for the torque-twist behaviour of a single prismatic length, only a decision about how several such lengths are combined -- end to end and each carrying whatever torque equilibrium assigns it, side by side and forced to twist together, or built in at both ends so that compatibility, not equilibrium alone, decides how the torque splits.

Derivation

Shafts in series -- a stepped shaft, or several distinct lengths keyed together end to end -- each twist by their own TL/(GJ) under whatever torque is applied to that particular length, and the twists simply add, because the far end's rotation is the sum of every span's individual contribution ahead of it. Where a span is driven in the opposite sense to its neighbour, its contribution to the sum carries the opposite sign, so a shaft that is twisted one way over part of its length and the other way over the rest can end with a smaller total twist than either part alone would show -- exactly the case a naive magnitude-only sum would get wrong.

Shafts in parallel -- two tubes on a common axis, keyed together at both ends so they are forced to twist through the same angle -- divide the applied torque in proportion to their torsional rigidities, by the same compatibility argument used for two axially loaded members sharing one elongation elsewhere in this app: equal twist plus torques summing to the total gives each shaft's share as its own rigidity over the combined rigidity of both.

A shaft built in at both ends with a single torque applied somewhere along its length is statically indeterminate: equilibrium supplies one equation relating the two end reactions to the applied torque, and compatibility supplies the second, because the two portions either side of where the torque is applied must twist by the same angle in opposite senses to keep both ends fixed. Solving those two conditions together gives each end's reaction torque in proportion to the stiffness of the portion nearer the other end, not simply in proportion to distance -- with equal rigidity on both portions this reduces to the torque splitting in proportion to the two lengths, but a step in the shaft's diameter partway along changes that split by more than a passing glance at the geometry would suggest.

φ=Σᵢ Tᵢ Lᵢ/(Jᵢ Gᵢ) (series, torque in each span given)
T_a/T=(GJ)_a/((GJ)_a+(GJ)_b) (parallel, common twist)

Assumptions

  • Every span or shaft in an assembly is itself prismatic; a continuously tapering shaft is outside this module's closed forms.
  • Series combination assumes each span's own internal torque is already known from equilibrium (or supplied directly); parallel and fixed-fixed combination instead assume the two portions are rigidly keyed so that they are forced to share a common angle of twist at the point they meet.
  • The fixed-fixed case assumes both ends are genuinely rigid restraints, contributing no rotational flexibility of their own.

M13   Torsion of Noncircular Prismatic Shafts core

Plane sections no longer stay plane. Two tabulated coefficients carry all the geometry.

τₘₐₓ=T/(α a b²) φ=TL/(β a b³ G) α,β=f(a/b)

Basis

Away from a circular cross-section, a plane section under torsion does not stay plane: it warps out of its own plane, and the amount of warping varies across the section, which is why the polar second moment that governs a circular shaft has no counterpart with an equally simple definition here. Saint-Venant's warping solution for the rectangle is exact -- an infinite series, not an approximation -- and two closed forms (the ellipse and the equilateral triangle) solve the same warping problem for shapes simple enough that the series collapses to elementary functions.

Derivation

The rectangle's twist coefficient and stress coefficient both come from the same warping-function series, expressed as a correction to the narrow-strip result that governs as the rectangle becomes very long and thin: the twist coefficient starts at one third for an infinitely long thin strip and is reduced by a term built from a rapidly converging sum over odd harmonics of a hyperbolic tangent, and the stress coefficient is that same twist coefficient divided by a related correction built from a hyperbolic secant sum. Both sums are evaluated directly rather than looked up, because the values printed in any table are three or four significant figures read off this same series at a handful of aspect ratios -- the series is the source, and a table is a compressed, lossy copy of it that also cannot answer for an aspect ratio between the tabulated ones.

Two shapes need no series at all because their warping function has a closed elementary form: the ellipse and the equilateral triangle. The ellipse's closed form is worth stating alongside the rectangle because, taken to the limit of equal axes, it reduces exactly to the ordinary circular-shaft torsion constant -- the one place in this chapter that an exact noncircular result and the elementary circular one are required to meet exactly rather than merely approximately, since a circle is an ellipse with equal axes and nothing about the physics changes at that special ratio.

τₘₐₓ=T/(α a b²) φ=TL/(β a b³ G)
J_ellipse=π a³b³/(a²+b²) J_triangle=\sqrt3/80 a⁴

Assumptions

  • The section is prismatic, solid, and one of the shapes given a closed form here: a rectangle (any aspect ratio at or above one), an ellipse, or an equilateral triangle.
  • The rectangle's maximum shear occurs at the middle of the long side, not at a corner -- the corners of a twisted rectangular section carry no shear stress at all, which is the detail this subject is most often remembered wrongly.
  • The series for the rectangle assumes an aspect ratio at or above one (the two sides are supplied as a longer and a shorter one); a square is the special case where the two coincide.

M14   Thin-Walled Closed Tubes core

Shear flow is constant around a closed loop, whatever the wall does.

τ=T/(2tAₘ) q=τ t=T/(2Aₘ) φ=(TL/(4Aₘ²G))∮ds/t

Basis

For a thin-walled tube closed all the way around, equilibrium alone -- without needing to know anything about how the material deforms -- requires the shear flow (shear stress times wall thickness) to be the same at every station around the loop: cut a short length of wall out at two stations and the difference between the shear flowing in at one and out at the other would have to be balanced by a stress gradient along the tube's axis that a uniform torque does not supply. Bredt's formula is that equilibrium statement, not a separate assumption about the material.

Derivation

Because the shear flow is constant around the loop, the torque it produces about any convenient point is that constant flow times twice the area enclosed by the wall's own mid-line -- a geometric fact about a closed curve, independent of the curve's shape -- which inverts directly to give the shear flow, and hence the shear stress at any station, as the torque over twice the enclosed area times that station's own local thickness. Where the wall's thickness varies around the loop, the stress varies inversely with it at each station even though the flow itself does not.

The angle of twist follows from equating the strain energy stored in the twisted tube to the work the torque does over that twist, using the shear-stress distribution just derived: carrying out that energy balance produces a formula built from a line integral of the reciprocal wall thickness around the whole loop, which collapses to a plain ratio of perimeter to thickness for a tube of constant wall thickness.

τ=T/(2tAₘ) q=τ t=T/(2Aₘ)
φ=(TL/(4Aₘ²G))∮ds/t

Assumptions

  • The wall is thin compared with the overall dimensions of the tube, so the enclosed area is well approximated by the area inside the wall's own mid-line rather than needing to distinguish an inner and outer boundary.
  • The tube is closed all the way around; a single lengthwise slit removes the equilibrium argument entirely and belongs to the open-section treatment elsewhere in this chapter, not this one.
  • The wall thickness supplied for the twist calculation is constant around the loop; a wall that varies in thickness needs the general line-integral form rather than the constant-thickness shortcut this module evaluates directly.

M15   Thin-Walled Open Sections in Torsion core

One slit along the length removes almost all the torsional stiffness. The comparison is the lesson.

J≈(1/3)Σᵢ bᵢ tᵢ³ τₘₐₓ=3T/(Σ bᵢ tᵢ²)

Basis

A thin-walled open section -- the same tube as the previous module, but slit along its entire length, or any section built from thin flat strips meeting along their edges rather than closed into a loop -- carries torque almost entirely by warping rather than by the closed-loop shear flow that governs a closed section, because a slit tube has nowhere for a continuous flow to run uninterrupted around. Its torsional stiffness is built up as a sum of narrow rectangular strips, each contributing the same one-third coefficient that governs an isolated long thin rectangle.

Derivation

Each strip of the open section is treated as a very long, thin rectangle in its own right, for which the exact Saint-Venant series elsewhere in this chapter has already been shown to converge to a torsion constant of one third of the strip's own length times its thickness cubed as the strip becomes long and thin -- exactly the regime every wall segment of an open thin-walled section is in. Summing that one-third contribution over every strip making up the section gives the whole section's torsion constant, and the peak shear stress follows by the same reasoning applied to whichever strip is thickest, since stress in this approximation scales with thickness while the constant scales with its cube.

J≈(1/3)Σᵢ bᵢ tᵢ³ τₘₐₓ=3T/(Σᵢ bᵢ tᵢ²)

Assumptions

  • Every strip making up the section is thin compared with its own length, so each one individually sits in the narrow-rectangle limit the one-third coefficient assumes.
  • The section is genuinely open -- no closed loop anywhere in it -- since a single closed cell changes the governing mechanism from warping to shear flow and this module's approximation no longer applies to that cell.
  • The strips meet along shared edges without additional stiffening at the junctions; the approximation does not separately account for the local stress concentration a junction between strips can produce.

M16   Helical Springs extended

A spring is a bar in torsion wrapped into a helix. The correction factor is where tight coils bite.

δ=8PD³n/(Gd⁴) k=Gd⁴/(8D³n) K_w=(4C-1)/(4C-4)+0.615/C, C=D/d

Basis

A close-coiled helical spring is, mechanically, a long circular bar in torsion, wound into a helix so that an axial load on the spring becomes a torque on the wire. Almost every quantity this module reports -- the deflection, the stiffness, the nominal shear stress -- follows directly from the plain circular-shaft relations elsewhere in this chapter applied to that wire; only the correction for how tightly the coil is wound is new physics, because a coil wound tightly enough stops behaving like a straight bar.

Derivation

Treating the coiled wire as an equivalent straight circular bar in torsion and applying the ordinary shaft twist relation, then converting the wire's angle of twist into the corresponding axial movement of the coil's free end through the coil's own geometry, produces the standard deflection formula in terms of the load, the coil diameter, the wire diameter, the number of active coils and the shear modulus; inverting it gives the spring rate -- load per unit deflection -- directly.

The nominal shear stress on the wire follows the same straight-bar torsion formula applied to the wire's own circular cross-section, but a real coil is curved, not straight, and curvature concentrates additional stress on the inside of the coil beyond what the straight-bar formula predicts -- more so as the coil is wound more tightly relative to the wire's own thickness. The Wahl correction captures that concentration as a multiplying factor on the straight-bar stress, built so that it approaches exactly one as the coil opens out and the spring index (the ratio of coil diameter to wire diameter) grows large -- the straight-bar approximation becoming exact in the limit where the coil's own curvature stops mattering, which is the check that the Wahl factor is a correction to the straight-bar result and not an unrelated formula that happens to look similar.

Combining several springs is a direct extension of the same series-and- parallel language used for shafts elsewhere in this chapter: springs end to end share the same load and their deflections add, so their flexibilities (reciprocal rates) add; springs side by side share the same deflection and their loads add, so their rates add directly.

δ=8PD³n/(Gd⁴) k=Gd⁴/(8D³n)
K_w=(4C-1)/(4C-4)+0.615/C, C=D/d

Assumptions

  • The spring is close-coiled: the helix angle is small enough that the wire's own torsion, rather than a mixture of torsion and direct bending, is what governs its behaviour.
  • The correction for coil curvature assumes the wire is thin compared with the coil -- a spring index at or above about three -- below which the derivation the correction rests on is no longer trustworthy even though the formula itself only diverges exactly at an index of one.
  • The material remains linear-elastic throughout the range of load considered.

Reading the result

A spring rate that seems too soft or too stiff is usually a wire-diameter error rather than a coil-diameter one, because the deflection depends on the wire diameter to the fourth power against only the third power for the coil diameter -- the single most sensitive dimension in the whole module.

Warning. The curvature correction diverges for very tightly wound springs.

M17   Composite Section Properties core

Build a section from a palette of primitives, add or subtract each, and read off every property the rest of the app needs.

A=Σ A_i ȳ=Σ A_i y_i/(Σ A_i) I=Ī+Ad² I_p=I_x+I_y r=√(I/A) S=I/c

Basis

Every property of a composite section -- however many pieces it is built from, and however many of those pieces are holes rather than material -- follows from three facts about integrals: an integral over a union of non-overlapping regions is the sum of the integrals over each region, a hole subtracts because removing material is integrating over the same region with a negative sign, and any second moment can be moved from one axis to a parallel one by the parallel-axis theorem once, given the primitive's own centroidal value. Nothing about assembling a real section needs a new principle beyond those three; it needs them applied correctly and consistently, which is where nearly all of the difficulty in this module actually lives.

Derivation

Each primitive shape in the app's palette -- rectangle, circle, hollow circle, right triangle, semicircle, quarter circle, ellipse, trapezoid, thin ring -- is a closed-form integral evaluated once and for all in its own natural frame, reporting its area, the location of its own centroid in that frame, and its second moments about its own centroid, never about the frame's origin. That last discipline is what keeps the parallel-axis shift from being applied twice, or not at all, by accident: because every primitive always reports a centroidal value, the composition layer that assembles a real section can apply the shift exactly once, in exactly one place, for every part regardless of shape.

Assembling a section from placed, and possibly rotated, primitives proceeds in three passes over the list of parts. First, each part's own area (carrying a plus or minus sign depending on whether it adds material or cuts a hole) is summed to get the total area, and each part's area times its own placed centroid location is summed and divided by the total area to locate the whole section's centroid -- ordinary composite-centroid bookkeeping, except that a "hole" is a negative area contributing a negative moment rather than a separate kind of object needing separate rules. Second, each part's own second moments are rotated about its own centroid to account for however it was turned when it was placed, shifted by the parallel-axis theorem from that part's centroid out to the whole section's centroid (not to the placement origin), and summed with the same sign convention. Third, the polar second moment, radius of gyration and section modulus follow directly from the assembled ix, iy and area. This screen also reports the first moment, about the assembled centroidal axis, of the area lying beyond a chosen level -- the quantity a transverse-shear check needs, taken here because it is a property of the section rather than of the loading, and described in full in the transverse-shear module.

The rotation step is worth its own remark because getting the sign wrong there is exactly the kind of mistake that can hide for a long time: the textbook transformation for a second moment is written for rotating the axes, and turning a shape by a positive angle is the same as turning the axes by the negative of that angle. A version of this code once carried the textbook's signs applied to the shape's own rotation directly, and it was wrong by as much as half the answer for a rotated right triangle -- wrong, and yet invisible on every square-on placement, because the sign only enters through a term that is exactly zero at zero and ninety degrees, and invisible again for any primitive whose own product of inertia happens to be zero (a rectangle, a circle, an ellipse), because only the product of inertia was affected, not the two second moments themselves. It surfaced only once a shape with a genuinely nonzero product of inertia, rotated by something other than a right angle, was checked against an independently integrated boundary rather than against another copy of the same formula.

A=Σᵢ signᵢ Aᵢ ȳ=Σᵢ signᵢ Aᵢ yᵢ/A
I=Ī+Ad² I_p=I_x+I_y

Assumptions

  • Every part is one of the palette's closed-form primitive shapes, placed and optionally rotated about its own reference point -- the point its dimensions are measured from, which is the centre of a rectangle, circle, ellipse or ring but the right-angle vertex of a triangle, the corner of a quarter circle, and the midpoint of the flat side of a semicircle or the bottom of a trapezoid; an arbitrary hand-drawn outline is represented on screen but its properties are still computed from the underlying primitives, not traced.
  • A hole is represented by a negative sign on an otherwise ordinary part, at the same placement a solid part of the same shape would use -- never by a negative dimension on the shape itself.
  • The assembled section's total area, after every addition and subtraction, is strictly positive; a description that removes more material than it adds is not a section this module will report properties for.

  • Closed-form summation and boundary integration of the same outline agree
  • The second moment of a rectangle grows as the cube of the depth

  • Total area <= 0 after subtraction — Not a number, an invalid section
  • A primitive with a zero dimension — Degenerate

Solution method

Every summation in this module -- the total area, both centroid moments, and all three assembled second moments -- goes through the same order-stable summation the rest of the app uses for any total built from terms that can differ by many orders of magnitude, specifically so that reassembling the same section with its parts listed in a different order reproduces every property to the bit rather than merely to a tolerance. That is not a cosmetic guarantee: the product of inertia of an asymmetric built-up section is often a small difference of much larger terms, where a plain running total can lose several digits depending on summation order alone, and a reader who reorders the parts of their own section and sees the numbers move has been shown something false about a shape that has not actually changed.

A second, independent path computes the same area, centroid and second moments by integrating around the section's own drawn boundary (a polygon, with each hole a second loop wound the opposite way) rather than by summing tabulated closed forms at all -- the same boundary-integration idea behind Green's theorem, carried out on the actual outline the screen draws. The two paths share essentially no code and no algebra, and requiring them to agree is the strongest check this module has: a section is drawn correctly and sums correctly by two routes that could fail independently, not simply computed once and trusted.

Limitations

  • The primitive palette is closed-form shapes only; sections that are not built from rectangles, circles, triangles and the other listed primitives -- and are not well approximated by combinations of them -- are outside this module.
  • Second moments are reported about axes through the section's own centroid, and about axes parallel to the reference frame the parts were placed in; this module does not itself search for or report properties about an arbitrarily oriented axis (that is the principal-axis module immediately following it).
  • A part's rotation turns it about its own reference point, so for a primitive whose reference point is not its centroid -- a triangle, a quarter circle, a semicircle, a trapezoid -- rotating the part also moves where its centroid sits; the composite section's overall orientation is fixed by however the parts were placed, not adjustable as a single further rotation of the whole assembly.

Reading the result

Three checks are worth making on any section built here, and all three are genuine tests this app runs continuously rather than folklore: the second moment about any axis parallel to the centroidal one is never smaller than the centroidal value itself, so a reported second moment smaller than what the same section gives about its own centroid signals an error in the section, not a legitimate result; the sum of the two second moments about any pair of perpendicular axes through the same point equals the polar second moment about that point, regardless of how the axes are oriented; and reordering the list of parts describing a section must never change any reported property, because a section is the shapes it is made of, not the sequence they happened to be typed in. A section whose reported area is implausibly small after a hole is added is worth checking for a hole entered with the wrong sign rather than the wrong size -- the two mistakes produce numbers of similar magnitude but very different meaning.

M18   Principal Axes and Principal Second Moments core

Rotate the axes until the product term vanishes. Structurally the same mathematics as the stress circle, deliberately written twice.

I_(1,2)=(I_x+I_y)/2±√(((I_x-I_y)/2)²+I_xy²) tan 2θ_p=(-2I_xy)/(I_x-I_y)

Basis

The second moments of area about a pair of axes through a point change as those axes are rotated, exactly the way the components of a plane stress state change as the cutting plane is rotated -- both are second-rank tensors in two dimensions, and the same rotation mathematics governs both, even though one describes a shape and the other a state of internal force. Rotating the axes until the product of inertia vanishes identifies a particular pair of perpendicular directions -- the principal axes -- about which the section's bending stiffness is, respectively, at its largest and its smallest.

Derivation

Writing the second moments about an axis pair rotated by an angle from the reference frame in terms of the reference frame's own ix, iy and ixy produces an expression built from the average of ix and iy, half their difference, and the product term, combined with the cosine and sine of twice the rotation angle. Setting the product-of-inertia expression to zero and solving for the angle gives the principal direction directly; evaluating the two second-moment expressions at that same angle -- or, equivalently, reading off the extremes of the rotated-axis expression directly -- gives the two principal second moments as the average of ix and iy plus or minus a term built from the same half-difference and product-of-inertia quantities that located the angle in the first place. This is the same algebraic structure as the plane-stress transformation used for the stress-and-strain chapter of this app, deliberately implemented as its own separate code rather than shared with it, because the two carry opposite sign conventions on their cross term -- one carries the product of inertia with a leading minus sign, the other carries twice the shear stress with a leading plus -- and a single shared implementation would need that sign passed in as a parameter, which is exactly the kind of parameter that gets passed incorrectly. Two independent implementations of the same mathematics, cross-checked against each other by a property test, cost less than one implementation and a sign convention nobody can be relied on to remember correctly at the call site.

I_(1,2)=(I_x+I_y)/2±√(((I_x-I_y)/2)²+I_xy²)
tan 2θ_p=(-2I_xy)/(I_x-I_y)

Assumptions

  • The input second moments and product of inertia are all taken about the same point -- ordinarily the section's own centroid, though the same rotation mathematics applies about any common point.
  • The section is treated as a rigid planar shape; nothing here depends on material properties, only on geometry.

  • This module transforms and extremises second moments already computed elsewhere; it does not itself assemble a section from primitives (that is the composite-section module) and does not decide which extreme value governs a particular bending problem.

M19   Plastic Section Modulus and Shape Factor extended

The fully plastic section splits at the equal-area axis, not the centroid.

Z=Σ |A_i ȳ_i| f=Z/S M_p=σ_Y Z

Basis

Once a section's most extreme fibre reaches yield, an elastic bending analysis has nothing further to say: the section is not exhausted at that point, because the material closer to the neutral axis has not yielded yet and continues to carry an increasing share of the moment as loading continues. The reserve between that first-yield moment and the moment at which the entire section has yielded is a purely geometric quantity, fixed by the shape alone and independent of the yield stress -- the shape factor.

Derivation

At full plasticity every fibre on one side of the neutral axis carries the material's yield stress in one sense and every fibre on the other side carries it in the opposite sense, so the internal force resultant is zero only if the two sides carry equal area -- which is why the fully plastic neutral axis is the axis that splits the section into two halves of equal area, not the centroidal axis a linear-elastic analysis uses, and the two coincide only for a section symmetric about that axis. The fully plastic moment is then the yield stress times the plastic section modulus, where the plastic section modulus is the sum of each half-area's own area times the distance from that half's own centroid to the equal-area axis -- the first moment of area of each half about the split, added together. For a rectangle, carrying that construction through gives a plastic modulus of one quarter of the breadth times the depth squared, against an elastic section modulus of one sixth of the same product, and their ratio -- the shape factor -- comes to exactly three halves: independent of the actual breadth, depth or yield stress, a fact that follows purely from the rectangle's shape and is one of the few numbers in this subject worth simply memorising and checking an implementation against, alongside a solid circular section's shape factor of sixteen over three pi.

Between first yield and full plasticity, a partly-plastic rectangular section has an elastic core -- a band about the neutral axis that has not yet yielded -- whose half-depth can be recovered directly from the applied moment in closed form, because for a rectangle the moment-versus-core-depth relation is a simple quadratic that inverts without a search: no root-finder is needed, and no root-finder means no bracket that could be set up wrong.

Z=Σ_i |A_i ȳ_i| f=Z/S M_p=σ_Y Z

  • The material is elastic-perfectly-plastic: it carries no stress increase whatsoever once yielded, up to the fully plastic state.
  • The closed forms this screen evaluates are for a solid rectangular section, and the plastic modulus of a solid circle is the other exact value the module carries; the shape factor of an arbitrary built-up section would need the same equal-area-axis construction carried out for that section's own geometry, which this module's closed forms do not attempt.
  • The section is loaded by pure bending alone, with no coincident axial force shifting where the equal-area axis actually falls.

M20   Reactions, Shear Force and Bending Moment core

Set the supports, list the loads, read the two diagrams. The flagship screen.

dV/(dx)=-w(x) dM/(dx)=V(x) V(x)=Σᵢ cᵢ ⟨ x-aᵢ⟩^nᵢ

Basis

A beam's reactions follow from the two equilibrium equations available to any rigid body in a plane: the sum of forces vanishes and the sum of moments about any point vanishes. Once the reactions are known, the internal shear force and bending moment at any cut follow from equilibrium of the piece of beam on either side of that cut -- and because every load this app accepts is built from a small set of standard pieces (a point force, a couple, a uniform strip, a linearly varying strip), the internal-force functions along the whole span can be written once, in closed form, rather than solved freshly at every position asked for.

Derivation

The reactions of a determinate beam -- one support pinned and the other a roller, or a single built-in end -- come directly from the two equilibrium equations applied to the whole beam and every one of its loads, each resolved to its own total force and the position that total effectively acts through (a distributed load's resultant sits at its own centroid, not at an endpoint). A structure needing a second compatibility condition beyond those two equations -- a propped or a fully built-in beam with too many supports for equilibrium alone to resolve -- is refused outright by name here, rather than quietly solved as if it were determinate, because the module that actually has the missing compatibility condition is a different one.

The internal shear and moment are then built from Macaulay's singularity functions -- a family of terms that switch on only past the position of whatever load contributes them, so that one closed-form expression describes the whole beam without needing a different formula on every span between loads. Every standard load type contributes its own known combination of singularity terms: a point force contributes one term to the shear, a uniformly distributed load contributes a term that switches on at its start and an equal, opposite one that switches off at its end (so that a strip covering only part of the span does not go on contributing beyond where it stops), and a linearly varying load needs one further term beyond that to capture the way its intensity itself changes along its length. The two diagrams were originally built from one shared piece of code parameterised by the load's own order, which is the version that dropped a factor of two on the shear contributed by a linearly varying load -- correctly getting both the reactions and the moment right while the shear alone was wrong, which is exactly the kind of error a shared, clever abstraction hides and writing the shear and moment terms out separately, one load type at a time, cannot.

Solution method

Every quantity is closed form, built from the sum of singularity terms described above and evaluated by direct substitution -- nothing here iterates to find a shear or a moment at a given position. Finding the peak moment is the one place a search genuinely happens, and it does not search for a single condition: the candidate set is the union of every zero of the shear (each bracketed within one smooth interval and found there, because a stationary point can only occur where the shear itself is continuous), every station where a load starts, ends or is applied, and both ends of the beam -- because a bending moment can peak exactly at a jump in the shear, under a concentrated load, and a search that only looks for a stationary point walks straight past that case, which is also the single commonest loading there is. Two further details keep that search honest: a stretch of beam where the shear is identically zero across an entire interval (the middle span of four-point bending, the standard way of producing pure bending) is recognised as having no isolated zero to report there rather than inventing one, and at every jump the diagram is evaluated from both sides, because the two values genuinely differ and averaging them, or picking one, reports a number that exists nowhere on the real beam.

Limitations

  • This module answers only for a single determinate span; a beam with more supports than equilibrium alone can resolve needs the indeterminate-beam module, which this one explicitly refuses to solve as if it were determinate.
  • The internal forces reported are for the beam's own centreline; no allowance for shear deformation, axial shortening, or a beam whose depth is not small compared with its span is made here.
  • Loads are resolved to their statically equivalent point of action for computing reactions and internal forces; this module says nothing about how a distributed load is actually transmitted into the material locally beneath it.

Reading the result

The two checks worth running on any result from this screen are also two of its own tested identities: numerically differentiating the moment diagram must reproduce the shear diagram away from any jump, and integrating the shear across the whole span must reproduce the total change in moment between the two ends. A shear or moment diagram that does not visibly return to zero at a free end, or whose reactions do not sum to the total applied load, signals a load entered outside the span or with the wrong sign well before it signals anything wrong with the diagrams themselves. Reading a value exactly at a support, a load or a couple needs the two one-sided values this module reports rather than a single number, because the true answer at that exact station is genuinely two different numbers depending on which side of the cut is being asked about.

M21   Propped and Fixed Beams core

One redundant reaction, one compatibility condition. Solve it two ways and the answers must match.

Basis

A propped cantilever or a beam built in at both ends has more reactions than the two equilibrium equations can resolve; the extra reaction (or, for a beam fixed at both ends, the extra two) is a redundant, and finding it needs a compatibility statement about how the beam actually deforms, not merely about the forces on it. The method used throughout this module -- the force method -- removes a redundant restraint, works out how far the released point moves under the real loads and under a unit value of whatever was released, and scales the unit case to cancel the real movement, restoring the condition the removed restraint was actually enforcing.

Derivation

Releasing the prop of a propped cantilever leaves a plain cantilever, which this app's determinate beam machinery already solves completely; that released cantilever deflects at the (former) prop location under the real loads, and deflects by some other amount there under a unit upward force applied at the same point. Because the prop's own real job is to hold that point at zero deflection, the actual prop reaction is whatever multiple of the unit force cancels the real-load deflection exactly -- a single division, not a search, because both deflections come from the same linear, already-solved determinate structure.

Releasing the restraint the other way -- removing the fixing moment instead of the prop -- leaves a simply supported beam with an unknown end moment still to be found, using the same idea applied to a rotation instead of a deflection: the released beam rotates at the built-in end under the real loads and under a unit end couple, and the actual fixing moment is whatever multiple of that unit couple cancels the real rotation. Both routes solve the same physical beam through two different intermediate determinate structures, and they are required to agree exactly -- a check that is genuinely independent of either route alone, because it is not merely running the same calculation twice, it is running two different calculations that both have to reach the same physical beam.

A beam built in at both ends carries the same idea one step further, with two redundants -- the two end moments -- released together, leaving a simply supported span, and two compatibility conditions (neither end may rotate) solved as a two-by-two linear system built from superposing the rotations a unit couple at each end produces on top of the real-load rotations. The familiar closed forms for a uniformly loaded, or a single point-loaded, fully built-in span are not a separate calculation from this general solver; they are kept alongside it specifically so that the general force-method solution and the closed form can be checked against each other on the cases where both exist.

  • A prescribed support settlement, where used, is a fixed geometric input decided independently of the loading, not itself a consequence of it.

M22   Flexure Formula and Section Design core

Stress varies linearly from the neutral axis, which passes through the centroid because it has to.

σ=-My/I σₘₐₓ=Mc/I=M/S 1/ρ=M/(EI)

Basis

A beam segment under pure bending, where plane sections remain plane and simply rotate relative to one another, stretches fibres on one side of some axis and shortens them on the other, with the strain at any fibre proportional to its distance from that axis. For a linear-elastic material the stress is proportional to the strain, so the stress too varies linearly across the section, and the location of the axis where it is exactly zero -- the neutral axis -- is fixed by requiring the stress distribution to produce no net axial force, which places it through the section's own centroid.

Derivation

Requiring the linear stress distribution to integrate to zero net axial force over the section is what fixes the neutral axis at the centroid in the first place -- a section bent about any other axis would have a net axial force appearing from nowhere, which pure bending does not supply. Requiring that same distribution to integrate, weighted by distance from the axis, to the applied bending moment is what fixes the constant of proportionality between stress and distance: the stress at any fibre is the moment times that fibre's distance from the neutral axis, divided by the section's second moment of area about that axis, with a sign fixed once and carried in the relation itself so that a sagging moment always puts the fibres below the neutral axis into tension. The peak stress at the extreme fibre, and the section modulus needed to keep that peak within an allowable stress, follow directly by evaluating the same relation at the extreme distance rather than at an arbitrary one. Curvature -- how sharply the beam's own axis bends -- follows from the same proportionality read as a statement about the whole cross-section's stiffness rather than about one fibre: it is the moment divided by the flexural rigidity, the product of the modulus and the second moment.

σ=-My/I σₘₐₓ=Mc/I=M/S
1/ρ=M/(EI)

Assumptions

  • Plane sections remain plane and perpendicular to the beam's own axis before and after bending -- the Euler-Bernoulli assumption, which holds well away from any point of concentrated load or abrupt change in section, and degrades where the beam is short and deep relative to its span.
  • The material is linear-elastic and the section is symmetric about the plane the load bends it in, so that a single second moment of area and a single neutral axis fully describe the section's bending response (an unsymmetric section needs the module immediately following this one).
  • The stress is evaluated at a cross-section away from where the load is actually applied or the support actually reacts, in keeping with Saint-Venant's principle.

M23   Unsymmetric Bending core

When the loading plane misses a principal axis the neutral axis is not perpendicular to it.

σ=-M_z y/I_z+M_y z/I_y (principal axes) tan α=I_z/I_y tan θ

Basis

The ordinary flexure formula assumes bending about a principal axis of the section; when the plane a load bends the beam in does not coincide with either principal axis, the bending response couples the two directions together, and the neutral axis is no longer perpendicular to the plane of loading. Whether that coupling can be ignored is not a matter of preference: it is decided entirely by the section's own product of inertia about the axes actually being used, which is a single quantity already available because it is one of the section-properties module's own outputs.

Derivation

Where the reference axes genuinely are the section's principal axes -- its product of inertia about them is zero -- bending about two axes at once simply superposes two independent one-axis flexure calculations, one term built from the moment and second moment about each axis, because with no product term there is no coupling between the two directions to account for. Where the reference axes are not principal, the two bending directions are coupled through the nonzero product of inertia, and resolving the stress needs the general two-axis flexure relation, built by solving the two coupled equilibrium conditions (zero net axial force, and the two moment balances) together rather than one at a time -- a relation whose determinant is exactly the same expression, second moment about one axis times the other minus the product of inertia squared, that also decides whether a section has a genuine principal direction at all in the section-properties module. Using the simpler principal-axis form on a section whose reference axes are not actually principal does not merely add a small error: because the coupling term is missing entirely, it can place the whole neutral axis in materially the wrong orientation, not merely report a stress that is somewhat off.

The angle the neutral axis makes with the horizontal follows, for the principal-axis case, from the ratio of the two bending terms scaled by their respective second moments -- and it is emphatically not simply the angle of the applied moment itself unless the two second moments happen to be equal, which is the source of the whole subject's most persistent intuition failure: a beam does not, in general, bend in the direction it is pushed.

tan α=I_z/I_y tan θ

  • The section's second moments and product of inertia about the chosen reference axes are already known (from the section-properties module) and supplied as inputs; this module does not compute them itself.

  • The material remains linear-elastic, and bending is the only action present at the point being examined -- combined loading with axial force or shear is outside this module.

M24   Composite and Flitched Beams core

Trade one material for an equivalent width of the other, then use the ordinary flexure result.

Basis

Two materials bonded together and bent as one beam must strain alike at their shared interface, and because stress is proportional to strain within each material's own linear-elastic range, the stiffer material carries proportionally more stress at the same strain. The transformed-section method exploits that directly: redraw one material as an equivalent width of the other, scaled by the ratio of their two elastic moduli, so that the whole beam can be analysed with the ordinary single-material flexure formula, and then convert the resulting stress back for whichever material was traded away.

Derivation

Requiring equal strain at the bonded interface and Hooke's law for each material together give the stress in the traded-away material as the transformed-section stress at that fibre multiplied by the modular ratio -- the whole method compressed into one multiplication once the transformed section's own flexure stress is known, and this is exactly the check that a transformation applied one direction and the other must agree: transforming to material one and to material two describes the same physical beam and must produce the same real stresses, even though the two transformed sections and their intermediate second moments differ by the modular ratio throughout.

The two geometries this module covers are not interchangeable, and the distinction is the heart of the module rather than a footnote to it. Two materials sitting side by side across the same depth -- a timber core faced with a steel plate on each side, the flitched joist -- transform into a single rectangle whose width is simply the sum of the real and the transformed widths, because both materials already share one depth and one neutral axis at mid-depth by symmetry. Two materials stacked one on top of the other with different depths -- a steel strap bonded under a timber plank, a slab cast onto a beam -- do not share a depth and are not symmetric, so their transformed section is two separate rectangles whose combined centroid, found the ordinary composite way from each layer's own transformed area, is generally not at mid-depth at all. Handing a stacked section's total depth to the side-by-side closed form answers for a different, symmetric beam that does not exist: on one worked timber-and- steel case doing so returns a second moment seven times too large, with every intermediate number along the way looking entirely plausible. Nothing in the side-by-side formula's own two arguments can detect that mistake, because both describe a legitimate section on their own terms; only which physical arrangement was actually meant tells the two apart, which is why the layout is an explicit, named choice on screen rather than inferred from the numbers entered.

n=E₂/E₁ b'=n b
σ₁=-My/Iₜ σ₂=-nMy/Iₜ

Assumptions

  • Both materials remain linear-elastic, and the modular ratio supplied is a genuine ratio of elastic moduli, not itself something this module derives from a material grade or code.
  • Which geometric arrangement applies -- side by side across a shared depth, or stacked with each material carrying its own depth -- is stated explicitly rather than inferred, because the two closed forms cannot be told apart from their inputs alone.

M25   Reinforced Concrete Beams extended

Concrete is assumed cracked below the neutral axis, so the depth of the compression block solves a quadratic.

b h'²/2=n A_st(d-h') I_cr=b h'³/3+nA_st(d-h')²

Basis

Concrete is taken to carry no tension at all -- once a fibre is calculated to be in tension, it is treated as cracked and simply absent from the section. That single assumption is what makes this module's whole method different from ordinary transformed-section bending: the extent of the effective section depends on where the neutral axis is, because the concrete above the axis counts and the concrete below it does not, so the axis cannot be found by an ordinary centroid calculation and has to be found as the root of an equation instead.

Derivation

Transforming the steel reinforcement into an equivalent area of concrete (by the modular ratio, exactly as in the general composite-beam method) and requiring the first moment of the resulting effective section -- the concrete above the trial axis, plus the transformed steel, treated as acting at the steel's own depth -- to vanish about that same axis gives a quadratic equation in the axis depth. Because the section's own extent depends on the answer being sought, this is not an ordinary centroid calculation solved by a single division; it has to be solved as a root-finding problem, though one with an exact closed-form root rather than one needing numerical search.

The quadratic has exactly one physically meaningful root: its two roots' product is always negative for physical inputs, so one root is always positive and the other always negative, and a negative depth is not a position inside the section at all -- it describes a point above the compression face, which is not a competing candidate to be discriminated against by a tolerance, it is simply not a place the axis can be. The root is therefore picked by an exact sign test rather than by comparing two positive candidates.

Getting that root arithmetically right matters more here than in most of this app's closed forms, because the ordinary textbook quadratic formula subtracts two nearly equal quantities exactly in the case of practical interest -- a heavily reinforced, comparatively shallow beam, where the transformed-steel term is large relative to the concrete geometry. Rewriting the same quadratic in the algebraically equivalent form that divides by a sum instead of subtracting removes that cancellation entirely, without changing which root is being computed.

Once the axis depth is known, the cracked second moment follows from the concrete's own contribution (a triangular stress block below the top face, contributing its rectangular second moment about the axis) plus the transformed steel's contribution at its own lever arm from that same axis -- an ordinary composite second moment, now that the effective section's extent is finally settled. Steel stress is the concrete-equivalent stress at the steel's depth, scaled back up by the modular ratio the transformation divided it down by in the first place: omitting that scaling factor is the single most consequential slip available in this module, understating the true steel stress by a factor on the order of eight to ten, always in the unconservative direction, because ordinary modular ratios for steel over concrete are themselves on that order.

b h'²/2=n Aₛₜ(d-h')
I_cr=b h'³/3+nA_st(d-h')²

Assumptions

  • Concrete carries no tensile stress whatsoever; every fibre below the neutral axis is treated as fully cracked and contributes nothing to the section's stiffness or strength.
  • The steel and the concrete are perfectly bonded, so that strain (and hence, through each material's own modulus, stress) varies linearly with depth across the whole effective section exactly as in an ordinary homogeneous beam.
  • The section carries reinforcement only in tension, at a single effective depth; compression reinforcement and any contribution from the concrete's own (small) tensile strength before cracking are both outside this module.

The rejected root of the governing quadratic is shown alongside the accepted one specifically so a reader can see it is not a close call being adjudicated by a tolerance: it is a depth above the top face of the beam, which was never a candidate position to begin with.

M26   Curved Beams extended

The neutral axis of a sharply curved bar sits inside the centroid, and the gap between them is where the arithmetic dies.

R=A/(∫ dA/r) R_rect=h/( ln (r_o/r_i)) σ=M(R-r)/(A r (r̄-R))

Basis

In a beam whose own unloaded axis is already sharply curved rather than straight, the ordinary flexure assumption -- that stress varies linearly with distance from the centroid -- stops holding, because fibres nearer the centre of curvature are shorter than fibres farther out even before any load is applied, so the same angular rotation of a cross-section produces a larger strain on the outer fibres than the inner ones. The neutral axis under bending is consequently not at the centroid at all; it sits slightly toward the centre of curvature, at a radius fixed by the section's own geometry through an integral of the reciprocal radius over the area.

Derivation

Requiring plane sections to remain plane (still true here, curvature alone does not violate it) while accounting for the fact that fibre length varies with radius rather than being uniform gives a stress distribution that is hyperbolic in radius, not linear, and locates the neutral radius at the section's area divided by the integral, over that same area, of the reciprocal of the radius -- a genuinely different quantity from the centroidal radius, which is the area-weighted radius itself rather than its reciprocal's weighted mean. For a rectangular section of constant width that integral collapses to the section depth divided by the natural logarithm of the ratio of the outer to the inner radius; for a circular section it collapses to a different, still closed, form built from the two radii's square roots.

Both of those closed forms are algebraically exact and numerically treacherous at the same time, because both describe an offset between two radii that are often close together relative to their own size, and the textbook forms compute that offset as an explicit subtraction. Rewriting the ratio of the two radii in terms of their fractional difference turns the logarithm into an inverse hyperbolic tangent of that small fraction, and the quantity actually needed -- the difference between that inverse hyperbolic tangent and its own argument -- is a power series in which every single term is positive. Summing that series, rather than subtracting two comparable quantities, computes the offset with nothing cancelling at any radius ratio at all; the circular section's offset is rewritten the equivalent way, turning a difference of two square roots into a quotient with the same difference in its denominator, which is the same technique applied to a different pair of nearly equal quantities. Both rewrites are checked against a reference computed independently at many-digit precision. Measured that way, and reading the ratio as the bar's mean radius against its own half-depth, the textbook subtraction has lost two of its sixteen digits by a ratio of ten, four by fifty, six by a hundred and nine by a thousand -- and a bar ten times deeper in radius than in half-depth is a curvature gentle enough to be an entirely ordinary question to ask.

A section that is not simply a rectangle or a circle -- a curved hook with a T, an I or a trapezoidal profile, which is the more common case in practice because the material that most needs to be there is at the inner fibre -- cannot be described by handing its inner and outer radii to the rectangular closed form: that closed form implicitly assumes the width does not vary between those two radii, and a section that actually does vary in width between them is a different, incorrectly described bar. This module instead builds every such section from radial bands whose width varies linearly between an inner and an outer radius -- constant width recovers the rectangle, a width that reaches zero at one end recovers a triangle, and stacking such bands recovers a T, an I, a channel or a trapezoid -- each band contributing its own closed-form area and flexibility integral, summed across the bands exactly the way primitive shapes are summed in the general composite-section module.

  • Where the section is not a plain rectangle or circle, it is described as a stack of linearly-tapering radial bands, which covers the common built-up curved-bar profiles but not an arbitrary curved boundary.

M27   Transverse Shear Stress core

Shear stress is zero at the free surfaces and peaks where the first moment does.

τ=VQ/(It) τ_(max,rect)=3V/(2A) τ_(max,circ)=4V/(3A)

Basis

A beam carrying a varying bending moment along its length is also, by the same equilibrium that relates the two, carrying a shear force -- and that shear force is not spread evenly across a cross-section the way an axial force is. Isolating a small slice of beam between two nearby cross-sections and requiring horizontal equilibrium of the material above some level within it shows that the shear stress at that level is fixed by the first moment, about the neutral axis, of exactly the material lying above it.

Derivation

The change in bending stress between two cross-sections a small distance apart, integrated over the area above some chosen level, produces a net horizontal force on that slice of material that must be balanced by a horizontal shear stress acting along the level in question; carrying that balance through gives the shear stress as the shear force times the first moment of the area above the level, divided by the second moment of the whole section and the width of the section at that level. Evaluating the general relation for a rectangle -- where the first moment above any level is itself a simple quadratic in that level -- produces the familiar closed form that the peak shear stress on the neutral axis of a rectangle is one and a half times the average shear stress, exceeding the naive force-over- area estimate because the shear stress is not uniform across the depth; the same evaluation for a solid circle, where both the width and the first moment vary as the level moves away from the centre, produces a different but comparably simple factor of four-thirds. Both closed forms are required to agree exactly with the general relation evaluated on the neutral axis of the same shape, which is a check between two paths to the same number that share almost no algebra.

Where the width of a section changes abruptly at some level -- the junction between a web and a flange in a rolled or built-up shape is the everyday case -- the general relation is evaluated with two different widths at that one level, because the width immediately above the junction and immediately below it are genuinely different numbers, often differing by an order of magnitude for a typical rolled section, and the shear stress computed with each is correspondingly different. Neither value alone describes the section at that level, and their average describes nothing physical at all; the module's own functions take the width as an explicit argument for exactly this reason, so that a caller who is asking about one particular side of the step is never handed the other side's answer by default.

τ=VQ/(It)
τ_(max,rect)=3V/(2A) τ_(max,circ)=4V/(3A)

Assumptions

  • The shear stress is assumed uniform across the width of the section at a given level -- a good approximation for a narrow, deep section and a progressively worse one as the section becomes wide relative to its depth.
  • The bending stress distribution the derivation starts from is the ordinary straight-beam linear one, so this module inherits the same Euler-Bernoulli assumptions the flexure module depends on.
  • At a step in section width, the reader is asking about a specific, named side of the step; the two shear stresses at that level are physically different quantities, not two estimates of one.

Reading a shear stress at a flange-to-web junction needs a decision about which of the two reported values is relevant to the check being made, not an average of the two.

Warning. Within one section depth of a load or support the stress distribution is not the one these formulas give.

M28   Shear Flow and Fastener Spacing core

Built-up sections stay built up only if the fasteners carry the flow between the pieces.

q=VQ/I s=n_f F_allow/q

Basis

A beam built up from separate pieces -- boards nailed together, plates riveted to form a girder -- behaves as a single beam only so long as the fasteners joining the pieces can actually transmit the horizontal shear that would otherwise let one piece slide relative to its neighbour. Shear flow is that same transverse-shear relation from the previous module, restated as a force to be carried per unit length along the beam rather than as a stress spread across a width, because that is the quantity a discrete fastener actually has to resist.

Derivation

Shear flow is the transverse shear stress at a given level multiplied by the width at that same level -- equivalently, and more usefully for this module's purpose, the shear force times the first moment of the area beyond the join, divided by the whole section's second moment, with no width term at all, because a fastener joint has a specific location rather than a continuous width to spread the flow across. Given how much force each row of fasteners can individually carry, the longest spacing that keeps the flow adequately resisted follows by direct division: enough capacity per unit length has to be provided at least as often as the flow demands it.

q=VQ/I s=n_f F_allow/q

Assumptions

  • The first moment used is taken about the same neutral axis as the whole built-up section's second moment, evaluated for the area on one side of the specific joint being checked, not for the section as a whole.
  • Fastener capacity is a single allowable force per fastener, applied identically to every fastener in a row; this module does not itself account for a fastener group's own stress concentration or uneven load-sharing.
  • The shear flow used for sizing is taken at the fastener's own location; where the flow varies along the span (because the shear force itself varies), the sizing at each station is a separate application of this module rather than one governing for the whole length.

  • Required spacing tightens in proportion to the shear force

M29   Shear Centre of Thin-Walled Open Sections extended

Load a channel through its centroid and it twists. The point that avoids twist is somewhere outside the section.

e_channel=b²h²t/(4I_z) e_(I, unequal)=h I_1/(I_1+I_2) e_(I, offset)=h²t(b_2²-b_1²)/(4I_z)

Basis

A transverse load produces a shear flow around a thin-walled open section that, taken together, is statically equivalent to a single transverse force -- but that equivalent force does not, in general, act through the section's own centroid. It acts through the shear centre, and a load applied anywhere else produces a twisting moment about that point in addition to ordinary bending, whether or not the person applying the load intended any twist at all.

Derivation

Locating the shear centre means finding the one point about which the shear flow's own moment matches the moment the equivalent single force would produce if it passed through that point -- equivalently, the point about which the shear flow's net torque, summed around the whole open section, is zero. For a channel, integrating the shear flow's moment contribution around the two flanges and the web and requiring the result to match a force acting at the (unknown) offset from the web gives that offset in closed form, built from the flange width and web depth squared, the wall thickness, and the second moment of the section about the bending axis -- and because the channel is symmetric about the axis running through the mid-depth of the web, the offset is measured purely along that one axis without needing a second coordinate at all. For an unequal-flanged I-section the same idea locates the shear centre along the web, splitting it between the two flanges in proportion to their own second moments about the bending axis, so that a section with equal flanges -- where the split is exactly even -- collapses to half the web depth, which is the same location the centroid already occupies. That degenerate case is not a coincidence requiring separate confirmation: it is the one configuration where the section is doubly symmetric and the shear centre is required, on general grounds, to coincide with the centroid regardless of the details of the closed form used to reach it.

Some sections need no calculation at all, because the geometry settles the question directly: an angle or a tee section is made of narrow legs each of whose own shear flow acts along a line through that leg, so the only point every leg's line of action can pass through simultaneously is the corner (or the flange-to-stem junction) where the legs meet, independent of any dimension.

e_channel=b²h²t/(4I_z) e_(I, unequal)=h I_1/(I_1+I_2)

Assumptions

  • The section is thin-walled and open (no closed cell anywhere in it), so its torsional and flexural responses can be treated as effectively decoupled the way this module's shear-flow argument assumes.
  • Where a closed form is given for a particular shape (channel, unequal-flange I), the section is exactly that idealised shape; a section merely similar to one of them is not guaranteed to share its exact shear-centre location.
  • A section with a genuine axis of symmetry has its shear centre on that axis by construction, independent of which of this module's specific closed forms, if any, also applies to it.

  • This module locates the shear centre; it does not itself compute the twisting moment or the resulting torsional stress a load offset from that point would produce, which belongs to the torsion modules elsewhere in this app.

The offset grows with how wide the flanges are relative to the web and shrinks as the web itself deepens, which is the geometric reason an open section built to resist twist is given a deep web and modest flanges rather than the reverse.

M30   Combined Axial and Bending; Core of the Section core

Move the load off the centroid far enough and one face goes into tension. The boundary is the core.

σ=P/A±Pec/I no tension while e≤r²/c rectangle: b/6×h/6

Basis

A load applied off the centroidal axis of a section is statically equivalent to the same load applied at the centroid together with a bending moment equal to the load times its own eccentricity, so the stress at any fibre is simply the direct stress from the axial load added to the bending stress the equivalent moment produces there -- ordinary superposition of two already-known relations, with nothing new in the mechanics beyond recognising that an off-centre load is a combined-loading problem.

Derivation

Superposing the uniform direct stress from the axial force and the linearly varying bending stress from the equivalent moment gives a combined stress that is still linear across the section, but no longer uniform and no longer guaranteed to keep a single sign: at a large enough eccentricity, the bending term at the fibre farthest on the far side from where the load sits can exceed the compressive direct term and put that fibre into tension. The core (or kern) of a section is the region within which the load's point of application can move while every fibre still stays in compression -- the boundary condition is that the extreme fibre's total stress just reaches zero, and setting the combined-stress relation to zero at that fibre and solving for the eccentricity gives the kern limit directly, as the square of the section's radius of gyration divided by the distance to the extreme fibre being checked.

That relation is written once, in general terms of the radius of gyration and the extreme-fibre distance, rather than as a family of separate formulas for a rectangle, a circle, and every other shape in the section library -- because the radius of gyration already encodes everything about a shape's second moment and area that the kern boundary depends on, the one general relation reduces to the familiar sixth-of-the-breadth result for a rectangular section and the eighth-of-the-diameter result for a circular one purely as arithmetic consequences of substituting each shape's own radius of gyration, rather than needing to be derived and coded independently for each shape in turn.

Where the eccentricity acts about two axes at once rather than one, the same superposition simply carries a second bending term built the same way, and the boundary of the region keeping the whole section in compression becomes a closed curve (a diamond, for a rectangle) rather than a single pair of limiting values -- traced directly from the same zero-stress condition evaluated at every point around the section's own perimeter, rather than from a separate formula for the biaxial case.

σ=P/A±Pec/I eₖₑᵣₙ=r²/c

Assumptions

  • The section remains entirely linear-elastic even where part of it is computed to be in tension; this module says nothing about what happens once any part of a no-tension material (masonry, unreinforced concrete, soil) actually would crack or separate.
  • The eccentricity is measured from the section's own centroidal axis, and the section's radius of gyration and extreme-fibre distance are already known from the section-properties module.
  • For a biaxially eccentric load, both bending terms act simultaneously about the section's own principal axes; an eccentricity referred to non-principal axes needs the unsymmetric-bending relation feeding into this one rather than the plain two-term superposition.

Limitations

  • The kern boundary is a property of the section's geometry alone; it says nothing about buckling or any other stability concern that might govern a slender compression member well before the kern limit becomes relevant.

Reading the result

The kern is best read as a design boundary rather than a strength limit: a load kept inside it guarantees the whole section stays in compression, which matters most for a material assumed unable to carry tension at all (masonry, an unreinforced footing), while a load outside it does not by itself mean the section has failed, only that part of it is computed to be in tension under the linear-elastic assumption this module makes throughout. The general kern relation converging on the familiar sixth-of-the-breadth and eighth-of-the-diameter results for a rectangle and a circle is a useful check in itself: any section's kern limit can be sanity-checked against those two familiar numbers by comparing its own radius of gyration to either shape's.

M31   Elastoplastic Bending and Residual Stress extended

Unloading from a partly plastic state is elastic, so the difference locks in as residual stress.

M_Y=σ_Y S M_p=σ_Y Z σ_res=σ_elastoplastic-σ_unload

Basis

Between the moment at which a section's extreme fibre first yields and the moment at which the whole section has yielded, a rectangular section carries an elastic core near the neutral axis sandwiched between two fully yielded regions near the extreme fibres, and the moment the section carries at any stage in between is fixed entirely by how deep that elastic core still is. Unloading from any such partly-plastic state is itself purely elastic -- the material does not un-yield, it simply relaxes along a straight elastic line from wherever the loading left it -- and the difference between the state at peak load and the state the same section would reach unloading elastically from that peak is what remains locked into the section as residual stress once the external moment is removed.

Derivation

Integrating the stress distribution of a rectangular section with a yielded region on each side, of a stated elastic core half-depth, and a linearly varying elastic stress within that core gives the moment carried as a simple closed function of the core depth; evaluating that function at a core reaching the full half-depth (no yielding at all) reproduces the ordinary yield moment, and at a core of zero (fully plastic) reproduces the fully plastic moment already established in the section-properties chapter of this manual, so the elastoplastic relation is required to span smoothly between the two moments this app already computes independently rather than introducing either endpoint as a fresh assumption. Because the moment-versus- core-depth relation for a rectangle is a simple quadratic, it inverts in closed form to give the elastic core depth directly from a specified moment, with no root-finding needed and, therefore, no bracket that could be set up incorrectly; a moment that would require more capacity than the section has even fully plastic is recognised directly rather than handed to a search that would fail to converge.

Residual stress after unloading follows from superposing the fully loaded elastoplastic distribution with an elastic unloading distribution scaled so that the two produce the same moment (since unloading removes exactly the applied moment, elastically) -- the residual is the difference between a distribution that has partly yielded and one that has not yielded at all but carries the identical moment. That difference is required to carry precisely zero net force and precisely zero net moment of its own, which is the necessary condition for it to be a self-equilibrating stress state left behind with no external load at all still applied -- exactly what residual stress physically has to be.

M_Y=σ_Y S M_p=σ_Y Z
σ_res=σ_elastoplastic-σ_unload

Assumptions

  • The material is elastic-perfectly-plastic, exactly as in the plastic-section-modulus module this one builds on, with no strain hardening at any stage of loading or unloading.
  • Unloading is assumed to remain entirely elastic throughout -- valid provided the reversed stress during unloading does not itself reach yield in the opposite sense, which is the usual case for a single load-unload cycle but not guaranteed for an arbitrarily large peak moment.
  • The closed forms given are for a solid rectangular section; a different shape's elastoplastic and residual-stress behaviour would need the same construction carried out for that shape's own geometry.

A residual stress that does not vanish at the section's own elastic-core boundary, or that fails either equilibrium check, signals an input outside where this module's single load-unload assumption actually holds rather than an error in the arithmetic.

M32   Plane-Stress Transformation and the Stress Circle core

Drag the angle and watch the element rotate, the circle point travel, and all three components move together.

σ_x′=(σ_x+σ_y)/2+(σ_x-σ_y)/2 cos 2θ+τ_xy sin 2θ σ_(1,2)=(σ_x+σ_y)/2±√(((σ_x-σ_y)/2)²+τ_xy²)

Basis

The three components of plane stress on one pair of perpendicular faces determine the stress on every other face through the same point; rotating the cutting plane does not change the physical state of stress at that point, only which components describe it. Requiring equilibrium of a small triangular wedge cut at an arbitrary angle from the original element gives the transformation relation directly, and every other quantity on this screen -- the principal stresses, the maximum shear, the stress circle itself -- is a property of that one transformation rather than a separate fact requiring its own derivation.

Derivation

Resolving the forces on a wedge element cut at an angle from the reference faces, and dividing through by the wedge's own inclined face area, gives the normal and shear stress on that face as the half-sum and half-difference of the two reference normal stresses combined with the reference shear stress through the cosine and sine of twice the rotation angle -- the factor of two arising because a stress component returns to its original value only after a half turn of the underlying tensor, not a full one, which is also why the graphical representation of this relation is a circle traversed at twice the physical rotation angle.

The principal stresses -- the extreme values the normal-stress transformation reaches as the angle varies -- follow by recognising that the transformation traces a circle in a stress plane centred on the average of the two reference normal stresses, with a radius built from the same half-difference and shear terms; the two principal values are simply that centre plus and minus the radius, and the maximum in-plane shear is the radius itself, reached exactly forty-five degrees of element rotation away from the principal directions -- a fixed relationship between the two special angles that falls directly out of the same trigonometric structure rather than needing to be derived separately for the shear case.

The principal angle is recovered from a two-argument arctangent of the shear term and the normal-stress difference, not from their plain ratio, because the ratio alone cannot distinguish the true principal direction from the one a half-turn away, nor place it in the correct quadrant. Where the two reference normal stresses are equal and the shear term is exactly zero -- a hydrostatic state in the plane -- the circle has collapsed to a single point: every direction produces the identical stress, and asking which one is "principal" has no more of an answer than asking which radius of a point is the longest. The module recognises that condition directly, scaled against the overall magnitude of the state so the judgement means the same thing whether the stresses are pascals or megapascals, and reports the direction as explicitly indeterminate rather than returning a zero that looks like an ordinary, meaningful angle or a not-a-number that explains nothing.

  • The state is one of plane stress: the two reference faces carry no out-of-plane shear, and the transformation describes rotation about the one axis perpendicular to that plane.
  • The material's response to the transformation itself needs no elastic properties at all -- transforming a known stress state to a new set of axes is a statement of equilibrium alone, true for any material.

M33   Absolute Maximum Shear Stress core

The plane of the element is not always the plane that fails. Ordering all three principal values decides it.

τ_abs=(σ_max-σ_min)/2 over {σ_1,σ_2,σ_3=0}

Basis

A state of plane stress has three principal stresses, not two: the two in-plane ones already found by rotating within the plane, and a third, equal to zero, in the direction perpendicular to that plane. The true maximum shear stress anywhere in the material -- on any plane, not only the ones containing the original loading direction -- is fixed by the largest and smallest of all three principal values, and the out-of-plane zero participates in that ordering exactly like any other principal stress would.

Derivation

For a general three-dimensional stress state, the maximum shear stress on any plane through a point is half the difference between the largest and smallest of the three principal stresses -- a standard three-dimensional Mohr's-circle result, applied here to the specific case where one of the three principal values is already known to be exactly zero because the state started as plane stress. Which pairing actually governs then depends entirely on the signs of the two in-plane principal values relative to that fixed zero: when both in-plane principal stresses share a sign, zero sits outside the interval between them, and the largest-minus-smallest spread running from the larger in-plane value down to zero (or up to zero, if both are negative) exceeds the spread between the two in-plane values themselves -- so the plane that actually governs is inclined out of the original plane of loading entirely, and the ordinary in-plane maximum shear understates the true answer, in the unsafe direction, by as much as a factor of two when one in-plane principal stress is much larger than the other. When the two in-plane principal values instead have opposite signs, zero already lies between them, and the in-plane pairing is the widest spread available -- the two calculations agree exactly in that case, which is the check made at the boundary between the two branches.

τ_abs=(σ_max-σ_min)/2 over {σ_1,σ_2,σ_3=0}

Assumptions

  • The state is plane stress, so the third principal stress is exactly zero rather than an independently specified value; a genuinely three-dimensional (triaxial) state with all three principal stresses nonzero needs the same sorting-and-spread idea applied to its own three known values, which this module's inputs already accommodate directly.
  • The two in-plane principal stresses supplied are already the true principal values (from the plane-stress transformation module), not arbitrary normal stress components on some unrotated pair of faces.

  • It assumes plane stress specifically (a genuinely zero third principal stress); a general triaxial state with three independently nonzero principal stresses uses the same sort-and-spread relation but is not, strictly, this module's own stated domain.

Reading the result

The single fact worth carrying away from this module is that the plane carrying the greatest shear stress in a material under plane stress is not always the plane of loading, and the case where the two in-plane principal stresses share a sign -- both tension, or both compression -- is exactly the case where a reader relying on the in-plane maximum shear alone will under-predict the true answer, never over-predict it. That asymmetry is why this module's result is always at least as large as the plane-stress transformation module's own in-plane maximum, never smaller, and checking that ordering is itself one of the strongest tests this pairing of modules has.

M34   Plane-Strain Transformation core

The same circle as for stress, provided the shear strain enters halved. That halving is the commonest error in the subject.

Basis

Strain transforms under a rotation of axes by the same mathematical structure as stress, because both are second-rank tensors in two dimensions -- but the strain tensor's off-diagonal (shear) component is conventionally reported as the full engineering shear strain, the total angle change, which is twice the tensor shear strain the transformation mathematics actually needs. That single factor of two, entered or dropped at the wrong place, is the most persistent single error in this part of the subject.

Derivation

The correct transformation is obtained, without deriving it as a separate piece of mathematics, by taking the stress-transformation relation already established and substituting the two normal strains for the two normal stresses and half the engineering shear strain for the shear stress -- this module's normal-strain and shear-strain transformations are implemented as exactly that substitution into the stress kernel, with the shear result doubled back at the very end to return to engineering strain convention. Requiring that substitution to reproduce this module's own results component for component is not an incidental check; it is the actual construction, made verifiable, and it is precisely the discipline that keeps the factor of one half from being applied twice, or not at all, in two places that could otherwise drift apart.

The principal strains and the maximum in-plane shear strain follow the same substitution, and the absolute maximum shear strain -- accounting for the out-of-plane direction exactly as the absolute-maximum-shear module does for stress -- needs no factor-of-two correction of its own, because by the point three principal strains are being compared, the engineering-versus-tensor distinction has already been resolved earlier in the calculation.

M35   Strain Rosette Reduction core

Three gauge readings go in, the full plane strain state comes out. The laboratory workflow this replaces takes five error-prone minutes.

ε_θ=ε_x cos²θ+ε_y sin²θ+γ_xy sin θ cos θ 45°: γ_xy=2ε_b-ε_a-ε_c

Basis

A single strain gauge measures one number: the direct strain along its own axis. Three gauges at three known, distinct angles at the same point measure three such numbers, and because the direct-strain transformation relation is linear in the three unknown components of the plane strain state, three independent readings are exactly enough to recover that whole state -- no more gauges are needed, and no fewer will do.

Derivation

Writing the direct-strain transformation relation once for each of the three gauges, at each gauge's own known angle, gives three linear equations in the three unknown strain components; collecting them into a three-by- three system and solving it -- by an explicit matrix inverse written out by hand, since the kernel this app shares between its two front ends imports no linear-algebra library at all -- recovers the full state from any three readings at any three distinct angles, not only the standard fixed-angle patterns. Two of the most common physical rosette layouts, gauges spaced forty-five or sixty degrees apart, have their own simpler closed forms that were long ago derived by hand for exactly those fixed angles; this module keeps both alongside the fully general solver specifically so the two can be checked against each other -- the general solver, evaluated at precisely the angles either standard layout uses, is required to reproduce that layout's own closed form exactly, which is a check between two genuinely different pieces of code arriving at the same numbers.

The system has a unique solution only when the three angles are actually distinct enough to be independent; two gauges at the same angle contribute the same equation twice, leaving only two independent equations for three unknowns, and a matrix that is singular, or too close to it, produces enormous but entirely spurious numbers if solved anyway rather than an honest absence of an answer. The determinant of the three-by-three system, normalised against its own largest entry so the judgement does not depend on the units the angles happen to be expressed in, is checked before any attempt to solve, and the whole calculation is refused -- not merely flagged -- the moment that check fails.

M36   Generalised Elastic Stress-Strain Relations core

Three normal components in, three out, plus the split of the stored energy that the distortion criterion depends on.

ε_x=(1/E)[σ_x-ν(σ_y+σ_z)] γ_xy=τ_xy/G u=u_vol+u_dist

Basis

For an isotropic linear-elastic material, each direct strain depends not only on the normal stress along its own direction but also on the two perpendicular normal stresses, through Poisson's effect -- stretching a material in one direction makes it contract sideways, so a stress applied in one direction produces strain in all three. The generalised, three- dimensional statement of Hooke's law captures that coupling directly, and the energy stored by a general three-dimensional stress state splits cleanly into a part that changes the material's volume and a part that changes its shape -- a split this chapter's failure module depends on directly.

Derivation

Each direct strain is the stress along its own axis, reduced by Poisson's ratio times the sum of the two perpendicular stresses, all divided by Young's modulus -- the ordinary uniaxial Hooke's law with the sideways contraction from the other two directions folded in as a subtraction. Shear strain, uncoupled from the normal components entirely for an isotropic material, is simply the shear stress over the shear modulus, with no Poisson term at all. Inverting these relations -- recovering stress from a known strain state -- is not merely running the same arithmetic backwards; it is built from the Lame parameters, and requiring that inverting a stress state to strain and then back to stress reproduces the original stress exactly is one of this module's own strongest checks, because the two directions use genuinely different formulas that happen to be exact inverses of each other rather than one relation solved twice.

The strain energy density stored by a general three-dimensional normal stress state splits into two physically distinct parts: a volumetric part, associated purely with the change in volume (the dilatation) and vanishing identically for any state that produces no volume change at all, and a distortion part, associated purely with the change in shape and vanishing identically for a hydrostatic state that changes volume without changing shape at all. The distortion part is written directly from the differences between pairs of principal stresses rather than as the total energy minus the volumetric part, specifically so that the identity the two are supposed to satisfy -- total energy equals the sum of the two parts -- remains a genuine, independently checkable statement about the physics rather than becoming true merely because one part was defined as whatever is left over after subtracting the other; and it is this same distortion energy, expressed as an equivalent stress, that the maximum-distortion-energy failure criterion in the next module is built on directly.

ε_x=(1/E)[σ_x-ν(σ_y+σ_z)] γ_xy=τ_xy/G
u=u_vol+u_dist

  • Poisson's ratio lies strictly within the physically admissible range; approaching its incompressible limit is a genuine physical condition (shared with the elastic-constants module earlier in this manual) rather than a numerically awkward but otherwise unremarkable input.
  • The three normal stresses supplied are already known at a point, referred to the same set of axes as the strains being computed; this module does not itself transform a stress state to a different orientation.

M37   Criteria of Failure core

Five envelopes on one plane, with the current state as a draggable point. The static figure in every textbook, made live.

σ_v=√((1/2)[(σ_1-σ_2)²+(σ_2-σ_3)²+(σ_3-σ_1)²]) σ_max-σ_min=σ_Y

Basis

A uniaxial tension test gives a single number -- the stress at which a material yields or fractures -- but a real structural point is rarely in a simple uniaxial state. A failure criterion is a rule for reducing a general, multiaxial stress state to a single equivalent stress that can be compared directly against that one uniaxial test result, and different, individually defensible physical arguments about what actually causes failure lead to different reduction rules -- which is why this module implements five of them side by side rather than choosing one as authoritative.

Derivation

Each criterion embodies a different physical claim about the mechanism that governs failure, and each reduces to a different combination of the three principal stresses. The maximum-normal-stress criterion claims that failure is governed by whichever single principal stress is largest in magnitude, appropriate to materials (typically brittle ones) that fail by simple fracture on the plane of greatest tension. The maximum-shear-stress criterion instead claims that yielding is governed by the largest shear stress anywhere in the material -- the same spread between the largest and smallest principal stress already computed by the absolute-maximum-shear module earlier in this chapter, applied here as a failure predictor rather than merely reported as a stress. The maximum-distortion-energy criterion claims that yielding is governed specifically by the shape-changing part of the stored strain energy established in the previous module, and is built directly from the same three principal-stress differences that module's own distortion energy uses, written the same cancellation-free way so that it is exactly zero, not merely small, for a hydrostatic state. The maximum-normal-strain criterion instead applies a strain-based limit, translated back into an equivalent stress through the material's own Poisson's ratio; and the total-strain-energy criterion sets a limit on the whole stored energy, volumetric part included, rather than the distortion part alone.

Every one of the five is required to reduce to exactly the applied stress itself under a simple uniaxial state -- the cheapest possible check on all five simultaneously, since a criterion that could not reproduce the very test result it is meant to be compared against would be internally incoherent. Where the five genuinely part company is under pure shear: the maximum-shear criterion there predicts yielding at exactly half the uniaxial yield strength, while the distortion-energy criterion predicts yielding at that strength divided by the square root of three -- roughly fifty-eight per cent of it rather than fifty -- and that fifteen-per-cent gap between two criteria, both applied to identical ductile metals, is not numerical noise; it is measured directly against real test data across the history of the subject, with the distortion criterion generally the closer fit for ductile metals and the shear criterion the more conservative, simpler-to-apply choice.

Two materials with unequal tensile and compressive strength -- the ordinary case for cast iron, concrete and other brittle materials -- are not well served by any of the five symmetric criteria, because those all treat tension and compression identically. The brittle envelope handles that directly, checking a two-dimensional stress state against a strength that differs by quadrant: a state where both in-plane principal stresses are tensile compares against the tensile strength alone, one where both are compressive compares against the compressive strength alone, and a mixed state -- one principal stress tensile, the other compressive -- compares against a straight line joining the two, drawn between the two different strengths precisely because that is the one quadrant where a symmetric criterion could not distinguish a material that fails unevenly from one that does not.

σᵥ=√((1/2)[(σ₁-σ₂)²+(σ₂-σ₃)²+(σ₃-σ₁)²])
σ_max-σ_min=σ_Y

Assumptions

  • The three principal stresses supplied already fully describe the state at the point being checked; this module does not itself compute them from a raw stress or strain state (the transformation modules earlier in this chapter do that).
  • The strain-based and total-energy criteria need the material's own Poisson's ratio in addition to a yield strength; the other three criteria do not depend on it at all, which is itself informative about what each criterion is actually claiming causes failure.
  • The brittle envelope assumes the two strengths supplied (tensile and compressive) are both positive numbers describing the material's actual, independently measured strength in each sense, not a single strength applied with a sign.

A hydrostatic stress state is the sharpest illustration available: the maximum-distortion-energy and maximum-shear criteria both predict no yielding at all, however large the hydrostatic stress grows, while the maximum-normal-stress criterion predicts failure at an ordinary, finite stress -- three criteria looking at the identical state and reaching qualitatively different conclusions, which is a genuine feature of the physics the five criteria disagree about, not an inconsistency in the implementation.

M38   Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels core

In a cylinder the circumferential stress is exactly twice the longitudinal one, always.

σ_hoop=pr/t σ_long=pr/(2t) σ_sphere=pr/(2t)

Basis

Where a pressure vessel's wall is thin compared with its own radius, the stress through the thickness of the wall can be treated as uniform and the radial stress -- which must vary from the internal pressure at the bore to zero, or to the external pressure, at the outer surface -- can be neglected beside the much larger stresses acting around the vessel's own surface. That single simplification, the membrane idealisation, is what separates this module from the exact thick-walled treatment immediately following it in this chapter.

Derivation

Cutting a cylindrical vessel with a plane containing its axis and requiring equilibrium of the internal pressure acting on the projected area of one half against the hoop stress acting around the two cut edges gives the hoop (circumferential) stress directly as the pressure times the radius over the wall thickness. Cutting the same cylinder instead with a plane perpendicular to its axis and requiring equilibrium of the pressure acting on the end cap against the longitudinal stress acting around the circumference gives the longitudinal stress as exactly half the hoop stress -- the same pressure and radius, but resisted by material running around a circumference rather than along a length equal to it, which is where the extra factor of two comes from. That the hoop stress is always precisely twice the longitudinal stress in a cylinder, for any pressure, radius or wall thickness whatsoever, is a direct and unconditional consequence of those two cuts sharing the same numerator and differing only in that one factor, not an empirical observation about particular vessels.

A sphere has no distinguished axis at all, so every cutting plane through its centre gives the same equilibrium statement, and its membrane stress is the same single value in every direction -- numerically identical to a cylinder's longitudinal stress at the same pressure, radius and thickness, because a sphere's cut is geometrically the same balance of pressure against resisting material as the cylinder's end-cap cut. The dimensional changes that follow -- how much the radius grows, how much a cylinder lengthens, how much the enclosed volume changes -- come from applying the material's own elastic stress-strain relation to the (uniform, biaxial or triaxial-by- symmetry) membrane stress state just established, each combining the hoop and longitudinal (or, for a sphere, the two equal in-plane) stresses through the material's Poisson's ratio exactly as the generalised Hooke's law elsewhere in this app would for any other biaxial state.

The identity worth holding onto from this module, because it is exact rather than approximate and holds for every cylindrical vessel regardless of its proportions, is that the hoop stress is always precisely double the longitudinal stress -- which is also the practical reason a cylindrical pressure vessel that fails from overpressure characteristically splits along a line parallel to its own axis rather than tearing across it: the hoop stress, resisting exactly that mode of failure, is the larger of the two by a fixed and unconditional factor. As the wall thins relative to the radius, this module's results must converge on the exact thick-walled solution to first order, and that convergence -- not merely agreement at one point, but the correct rate of approach as the ratio grows -- is one of the strongest checks tying this module to the one that follows it.

Warning. Thin-wall assumption degrades below a radius-to-thickness ratio of ten; the thick-walled solution is the honest one here.

M39   Thick Cylinders and Spheres extended

Two constants fixed by the two pressures. The equilibrium equation it satisfies is the strongest test in the whole app.

σ_r=A-B/r² σ_θ=A+B/r² σ_r(r_i)=-p_i, σ_r(r_o)=-p_o

Basis

Where a cylinder's wall is too thick for the membrane idealisation to be trusted, the radial stress can no longer be neglected, and the full elasticity solution shows that both the radial and the hoop stress at any radius are made up of a constant part and a part that varies as the inverse square of the radius -- two constants fixed entirely by the two boundary conditions at the inner and outer surfaces, where the radial stress must equal minus whatever pressure acts there.

Derivation

Solving the equilibrium equation for a thick-walled cylinder under axisymmetric pressure, together with the compatibility condition that relates radial displacement to both strains, produces a stress field with exactly this constant-plus-inverse-square form; the two constants are then found from the two boundary conditions, that the radial stress must exactly equal the negative of the internal pressure at the bore and the negative of the external pressure at the outer surface -- two linear equations in the two unknown constants, solved directly rather than searched for. The resulting hoop stress is always largest at the bore and falls monotonically outward under internal pressure alone, which is the direct reason a thick pressure vessel's material is used most efficiently near its inner surface and least efficiently near its outer one.

The single strongest test available anywhere in this module -- and, for that matter, one of the strongest in the whole app -- is that this closed form was not merely fitted to satisfy the two boundary conditions; it also has to satisfy the equilibrium equation itself at every radius in between, a differential statement the closed form was never explicitly told to obey. Differentiating the shipped stress functions numerically and substituting them back into that equilibrium equation checks the entire derivation at once, rather than only the two endpoints, which is precisely the kind of check that a closed form satisfying its boundary conditions by construction but containing an algebraic slip somewhere in the middle would fail.

A compound (shrink-fit) cylinder -- an outer sleeve heated, slipped over an inner cylinder, and allowed to cool so that it clamps down with a deliberate, controlled interference -- generates a contact pressure at the join that follows directly from requiring the two cylinders' own radial displacements, computed independently by the same thick-cylinder relations applied to each as its own separate body, to differ by exactly the interference machined in. Because that relation is linear in the interference, the reverse question -- how much interference is needed to produce a target contact pressure -- is not solved by any search either: a single evaluation at unit interference gives the proportionality constant directly, and scaling by the target pressure gives the answer, guaranteeing by construction that the forward and reverse relations can never disagree with each other, since they are the same formula evaluated twice rather than two independently derived ones.

The same governing equations, worked through for spherical rather than cylindrical symmetry, give a stress field of the same constant-plus-inverse- power form but with the inverse cube of the radius rather than the inverse square, reflecting the extra dimension of symmetry a sphere has over a cylinder.

σ_r=A-B/r² σ_θ=A+B/r²
σᵣ(rᵢ)=-pᵢ σᵣ(rₒ)=-pₒ

  • The shrink-fit relations assume both cylinders are of the same material (sharing a single elastic modulus) with the interference already machined in as a controlled, known radial misfit.

M40   Rotating Discs, Rings and Cylinders extended

A pinhole at the centre doubles the peak stress. The limit is genuinely discontinuous, so it is offered as two cases.

σ_θ=ρω²[((3+ν)/8)(r_o²+r_i²+r_i²r_o²/r²)-((1+3ν)/8)r²] σ_(θ,ring)=ρω²r²

Basis

A disc spinning about its own axis carries hoop and radial stress generated entirely by its own inertia -- every particle of the disc requires a centre-seeking force to keep it moving in a circle, and that force is supplied by the stress in the material around and beside it, exactly the way internal pressure supplies the force needed to contain a pressurised vessel. The governing equilibrium equation has the same mathematical shape as the thick-walled cylinder's, with the centrifugal body force appearing as an additional term, and the boundary conditions -- zero radial stress at any genuinely free edge -- again fix the constants of the solution.

Derivation

Solving the disc's own equilibrium equation, now carrying the centrifugal body force proportional to the material's density and the square of the angular speed, with the boundary condition that the radial stress vanishes at a free outer edge (and, where present, at a free inner bore as well), gives closed forms for the radial and hoop stress built from the same material and geometric parameters that govern a thick cylinder, evaluated under a body-force loading rather than a boundary-pressure one. A thin ring, so slender in the radial direction that it can be treated as carrying essentially only hoop stress and no meaningful radial stress at all, is the simplest limiting case of the same physical mechanism, and its hoop stress reduces to the density times the square of both the speed and the radius -- recoverable, as a sanity check, from the general disc relation in the limit of a vanishingly thin annulus.

The single most important physical fact about this module is that the solid disc and the disc with a central hole are not the same solution taken to two different limits of one continuous parameter: as the hole in a pierced disc shrinks toward nothing, the hoop stress at the bore rises to exactly twice the stress at the centre of an equivalent solid disc, a genuinely discontinuous jump rather than a smooth approach. The mathematical reason is that a solid disc's own boundary condition -- stress finite at the centre, where there is no free surface at all -- differs fundamentally from a pierced disc's boundary condition of a free, stress-relieving inner edge, however small; a free inner edge, no matter how close to the axis, is still a free edge, and a disc with no hole has no such edge to relieve stress at all. Because the underlying boundary conditions are qualitatively different, not merely quantitatively close, the two closed forms are kept as genuinely separate functions in this app rather than as one function whose radius argument happens to include zero, and a single unified function would silently return whichever answer its author happened to code for the degenerate case -- almost certainly the wrong one, since the physically correct jump cannot be produced by any continuous formula at all.

A profile of constant strength -- a disc shaped so that the hoop stress is the same design value at every radius rather than peaking at the centre or the bore -- is not found by trial and error over a family of shapes; it follows directly from requiring the equilibrium equation to hold with the hoop stress held constant, which forces the disc's own thickness to vary exponentially with the square of the radius, thickest at the centre and thinnest at the rim.

σ_θ=ρω²[((3+ν)/8)(r_o²+r_i²+r_i²r_o²/r²)-((1+3ν)/8)r²]
σ_(θ,ring)=ρω²r²

Assumptions

  • The disc is thin enough, in the direction of its own spin axis, that the stress can be treated as uniform through that thickness -- an axisymmetric plane-stress idealisation, not a fully three-dimensional one.
  • The disc spins at a steady angular speed; nothing here accounts for the transient stress during acceleration or deceleration, or for any resonance with a structure's own natural frequencies.
  • A central hole, where present, is genuinely free of any applied pressure at its own inner edge; a pierced disc pressed onto a shaft with an interference fit, as in a shrink-fit assembly, needs that contact pressure added as a further boundary condition rather than being treated as this module's plain free-bore case.

M41   Deflection by Integration core

Integrate the moment twice with singularity terms and the boundary conditions fix the constants.

EId²v/(dx²)=M(x) ∫ ⟨ x-a⟩ⁿdx=(⟨ x-a⟩ⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1)

Basis

For a beam whose curvature is small, the curvature of its deflected shape is directly proportional to the bending moment at that point and inversely proportional to the flexural rigidity -- the same relation the flexure-stress module uses to relate moment to curvature at a cross-section, now read as a differential equation governing the whole shape of the beam's deflected axis. Integrating that equation twice, with two constants of integration fixed by the beam's own boundary conditions, gives the slope and deflection at every station along the span.

Derivation

Because the bending moment along any beam this app represents is already available as an exact sum of Macaulay singularity terms, integrating the governing curvature equation is not a numerical procedure at all: each term c <x-a>^n integrates to exactly c <x-a>^(n+1)/(n+1), with nothing approximated, so integrating the whole moment expression once gives the slope up to a constant and integrating it again gives the deflection up to two constants, both still expressed as sums of singularity terms rather than as numbers sampled on a grid. Sampling the moment diagram numerically and integrating it that way would only approximate a quantity already known exactly, and would blur precisely the discontinuities in slope and curvature that a stepped or discontinuously loaded beam genuinely has.

The two constants of integration are fixed by exactly the same information the reactions were fixed by: a simply supported beam pins the deflection to zero at both supports, giving two equations for the two constants directly, while a cantilever pins both the deflection and the slope to zero at the single built-in end. This is also why a beam needing a third support cannot be solved by this module at all -- there would be a third condition and still only two constants, exactly the same indeterminacy the reactions module refuses to resolve, for the same underlying reason.

Locating the point of maximum deflection needs a genuine search, but a simpler one than finding the peak moment: because the slope is continuous even at a concentrated load (only the curvature, one derivative further, jumps there), a stationary point of the deflection is always a genuine, single-valued zero of the slope, with no two-sided values to worry about. What still must not be dropped is the two ends of the beam, because a cantilever's largest deflection is at its free end, where the slope generally does not vanish at all -- a search that only looked for a stationary point would report zero for the single most common case in the whole subject.

EI v''=M(x) EI v'''=V(x) EI v''''=-w(x)

Assumptions

  • Deflections and slopes are small enough that curvature can be approximated by the second derivative of the deflection, rather than the full nonlinear curvature expression -- the ordinary small-deflection beam theory this entire chapter shares.
  • The flexural rigidity is constant along whatever stretch of beam is being integrated over; a beam whose section changes along its length needs the integration carried out piecewise, matching slope and deflection at the change.
  • The beam is determinate in the same sense the reactions module requires: exactly two boundary conditions are available to fix the two constants of integration, no more and no fewer.

M44   Moment-Area and Conjugate Beam extended

The area under the curvature diagram is the change in slope; its first moment is the deviation.

θ_BA=∫_A^B(M/(EI))dx t_BA=∫_A^BM/(EI) x̄ dx

Basis

The curvature diagram along a beam -- bending moment divided by flexural rigidity, station by station -- carries geometric meaning of its own, independent of solving any differential equation directly: the area under that diagram between two stations is exactly the change in slope between them, and the first moment of that same area about a chosen station is exactly how far one point on the deflected beam sits from the tangent line drawn at another. Those two facts, the two moment-area theorems, turn a deflection calculation into a problem of areas and centroids on a diagram already available from the moment calculation.

Derivation

Both moment-area theorems follow directly from the same governing differential equation the general integrator uses, merely read geometrically rather than solved algebraically: integrating curvature between two stations gives the change in slope by the fundamental theorem of calculus, which is precisely the statement that the area under the curvature diagram between those stations equals that change; integrating curvature weighted by distance from a reference station gives the tangential deviation the same way. Because these are the same governing equation as the general integrator's, not a different piece of physics, the answer either theorem produces is required to agree with direct integration on the same beam -- a check between two constructions that share the underlying equation but essentially no code, since one solves a boundary- value problem for two constants of integration and the other accumulates areas and first moments directly.

Which of a deviation's two stations is the one "sitting at a distance from the tangent" and which is the one "the tangent is drawn at" is not a symmetric question -- swapping the two changes which theorem is actually being applied -- and it is, by the account carried in this app's own development history, the single easiest thing to get backwards in this part of the subject; the two stations are therefore named explicitly in the functions themselves (which station's deviation is being measured, and which station the tangent is drawn at) rather than left as an ordered pair whose order a caller has to remember correctly. Applying the two theorems between the two supports of a simply supported beam, and combining the resulting deviation with simple proportion along the span, gives the actual deflection at any station directly -- and the conjugate-beam method, taught separately in most courses as loading a fictitious beam with the real beam's own curvature diagram and reading its shear and moment as the real beam's slope and deflection, is, in this app, mathematically the identical construction carried out by the identical code: a different way of explaining the same two moment-area theorems, not a third independent method.

θ_BA=∫_A^B(M/(EI))dx t_BA=∫_A^BM/(EI) x̄ dx

  • As with every other deflection module in this chapter, small-deflection, linear-elastic beam theory applies throughout.

Solution method

Both integrals -- the plain area for the slope-change theorem and the first-moment integral for tangential deviation -- are evaluated by four-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature applied piecewise between every station where the beam's own loading changes character. This is not an approximation with a hidden tolerance: between any two such stations the bending moment is a polynomial of degree at most three, so the product the tangential-deviation integral needs is of degree at most six, and four-point Gauss-Legendre integrates any polynomial up to degree seven exactly -- to the last bit double-precision arithmetic can represent, not merely to within some accepted error. Sampling the same integral on a uniform grid instead would converge only slowly across the diagram's own discontinuities and would need a stated tolerance to hide how well or poorly it was actually doing.

A deflection from this module that appears to differ from the integrator's by a bare sign, rather than by a genuine discrepancy, is worth checking against that single flip point before concluding anything is wrong.

M45   Energy Methods for Deflection extended

Differentiate the stored energy with respect to the load whose deflection is wanted.

δᵢ=∂ U/(∂ Pᵢ)=∫(M/(EI))∂ M/(∂ Pᵢ) dx δ=ΣN n L/(AE)

Basis

Castigliano's theorem states that the deflection at the point a load is applied, in the direction of that load, is the rate at which the beam's total stored strain energy grows as that load itself grows -- differentiate the energy with respect to the load, and the result is the deflection. Carried out for a bending member, that differentiation is mathematically identical to the unit-load (virtual work) method: introduce a fictitious unit load at the point and in the direction whose deflection is wanted, and integrate the product of the real bending-moment diagram and the diagram that unit load alone would produce.

Derivation

Differentiating the strain energy stored in bending -- itself an integral of the bending moment squared, divided by twice the flexural rigidity -- with respect to one particular applied load, and carrying that differentiation inside the integral, produces an integral of the real bending moment multiplied by the rate of change of the moment with respect to that one load; and because the bending moment diagram is linear in every applied load individually, that rate of change is exactly the moment diagram a unit value of that same load, applied alone, would produce. The two named methods are consequently not two separate pieces of mechanics implemented twice in this app: they are one integral, computed by one function, reached by two different arguments for why it is the deflection being sought. The same idea extends directly to a pin-jointed frame's axial members, where the analogous integral collapses to a simple sum over the members, each contributing its own real force times the corresponding unit force, times its length, divided by its own axial stiffness -- the truss-analysis counterpart of the same virtual-work idea, with force replacing moment and a sum replacing an integral because each member carries a single, constant axial force along its whole length rather than a varying moment.

Getting the sign of that fictitious unit load right is worth stating plainly, because it is the one place the method can go quietly wrong without producing an obviously implausible number: the integral is a work, and it equals the deflection only when the unit load used to generate the second moment diagram is genuinely of unit magnitude in the assumed positive sense. A unit load entered as -1 instead of +1 produces an integral with its sign simply reversed, and nothing in the arithmetic itself signals that anything is wrong -- which is precisely why this distinction is stated directly in this module's own account of itself rather than left as an assumption a caller might not realise they were required to honour.

Where a deflection is wanted at a point carrying no real load at all -- the everyday case for finding the deflection under a bare span with the loads elsewhere -- the unit load is introduced as a genuinely fictitious addition to the real structure and the resulting integral is evaluated with the real structure's own loading, never by attempting to divide by an applied load of zero; the "dummy load" language some treatments use for this describes exactly that fictitious unit load, introduced symbolically and never actually needing to be carried to zero or divided into anything.

δᵢ=∂ U/(∂ Pᵢ)=∫(M/(EI))∂ M/(∂ Pᵢ) dx
δ=ΣN n L/(AE)

Assumptions

  • The structure remains linear-elastic, so that the bending-moment diagram from several loads is the sum of each load's own diagram and the energy differentiation that Castigliano's theorem depends on is valid.
  • The unit load used for the virtual-work integral is genuinely of magnitude one, in the same sense (and, where relevant, the same units) as the real load whose corresponding deflection is being sought, applied at the exact point and in the exact direction that deflection is measured.
  • The truss-member summation assumes each member carries a single, constant axial force along its own length, with real and unit member forces already known from a separate structural analysis this module does not itself perform.

M46   Buckling of Ideal Columns core

A slender column fails by going sideways long before the material yields. Which way it goes is a decision the user must see.

P_cr=π²EI/(KL)² σ_cr=π²E/(KL/r)² (KL/r)_c=√(2π²E/σ_Y)

Basis

A perfectly straight, centrally loaded, perfectly elastic column carries increasing axial load with no sideways movement at all until, at one particular load, a sideways deflected shape becomes possible in equilibrium alongside the straight one -- and below that load, the straight shape is the only equilibrium state, while at and above it, an infinitesimal sideways nudge finds no restoring tendency to return from. That load is the critical (Euler) load, and it depends on the column's bending stiffness and its length between points of zero moment, not on its material strength at all.

Derivation

Writing the equilibrium of a laterally deflected column under a compressive axial load -- the axial load acting through the sideways deflection produces a bending moment proportional to that same deflection, which is the destabilising mechanism itself -- gives a differential equation whose solution is sinusoidal, and requiring that sinusoid to satisfy the actual end conditions (zero deflection at both ends, for the simplest case) admits a nontrivial solution only at specific discrete loads, the smallest of which is the critical load: proportional to the flexural rigidity and inversely proportional to the square of the column's own length. A column free at one end and only restrained at the other reaches its own critical condition at exactly the same load as a pinned-pinned column of twice its own length, because the shape it deflects into between its restraint and an imagined mirror image beyond the free end is exactly half of the pinned-pinned column's own sinusoid -- which is the physical content behind the effective-length factor: each of the four classical end conditions is assigned a factor that expresses its own critical length as some multiple of an equivalent pinned-pinned column's length, and the critical load and critical stress are then the same formulas the ordinary pinned-pinned column uses, evaluated at that equivalent length rather than the physical one. The four classical factors' own critical loads, compared to each other at the same physical length and material, come out in the fixed ratio one to four to a little over two to one-quarter -- a purely geometric consequence of the four factors' own defining ratios, checkable independently of any column's actual dimensions.

Because the critical stress this relation predicts grows without bound as the column shortens, it eventually exceeds any real material's yield strength -- a physically meaningless prediction, since a short column crushes or yields long before an infinite stress could be reached. The slenderness ratio at which the ideal critical stress exactly equals the yield stress marks the boundary of where this module's own idealisation remains applicable at all; below that slenderness, the column's true behaviour has departed from the elastic (Euler) curve and belongs instead to the inelastic column module immediately following this one in the chapter.

Where a column is braced differently about its two principal axes -- lateral support along one axis that the other axis does not share, the ordinary case for a column braced by girts or sheeting running one way only -- the axis that actually governs is not necessarily the one with the smaller second moment of area; it is whichever axis gives the smaller critical load, and because the two axes can have different effective lengths as well as different second moments, that comparison has to be made explicitly, axis by axis, rather than assumed from the second moments alone. Comparing only the two second moments and picking the smaller is correct only when both axes share the same effective length, and is wrong in the unsafe direction -- overstating the column's true capacity -- the moment they do not.

P_cr=π²EI/(KL)² σ_cr=π²E/(KL/r)²
(KL/r)_c=√(2π²E/σ_Y)

Assumptions

  • The column is perfectly straight before loading, perfectly centrally loaded, and made of a linear-elastic material with no imperfection of any kind -- the ideal case this whole module is named for, and the reason a real column never quite reaches the load this module predicts.
  • The effective-length factor used matches the column's actual end restraint; the four classical values (pinned-pinned, fixed-fixed, fixed-pinned, fixed-free) are idealised limiting cases, and a real connection's restraint usually falls somewhere between two of them.
  • The slenderness ratio is at or above the critical slenderness at which the ideal elastic curve and the material's own yield strength coincide; below that ratio this module's own applicability guard signals that the ideal curve no longer describes the column's true behaviour.

M47   Eccentrically Loaded Columns core

The one relation in the app that cannot be inverted in closed form. Finding the allowable load needs a bracketed root search.

σₘₐₓ=(P/A)[1+ec/r² sec ((L/(2r))√(P/(EA)))] δ=e[ sec ((L/(2r))√(P/(EA)))-1]

Basis

A column loaded even slightly off its own centroidal axis begins bending from the very first increment of load, rather than remaining perfectly straight until a sudden bifurcation as the ideal column does -- the eccentric moment and the resulting deflection amplify each other progressively as the axial load grows, and the peak combined stress from that direct load and its own self-amplifying bending grows without bound as the load approaches the same ideal critical load the previous module established, even though no sudden bifurcation occurs.

Derivation

Solving the beam-column differential equation for a pin-ended member carrying a constant eccentricity at both ends -- the same governing equation as the ideal column, now carrying a particular, nonzero boundary eccentricity rather than admitting only the trivial straight solution -- gives a deflected shape whose maximum, at mid-height, is proportional to a secant of an angle built from the load, the length and the column's own bending stiffness. The peak combined stress -- direct stress plus the bending stress from that amplified mid-height moment -- follows directly by substituting the resulting deflection back into the ordinary combined axial-and-bending stress relation, producing the single closed-form expression that is the whole content of this module: a family of curves, one for each value of the eccentricity ratio, all of them sharing the identical vertical asymptote at the same ideal critical load the previous module derived, because the secant's own argument reaches a quarter turn -- where the secant itself diverges -- at exactly that load, independent of how large or small the eccentricity is.

This is the one relation in the whole app that genuinely cannot be rearranged to solve for the load directly given a target stress: the load appears both outside the secant and inside its own argument, transcendentally, and no algebraic manipulation removes that. Finding the allowable load is therefore the one place in this entire application where a bracketed root search is unavoidable rather than a matter of numerical convenience, and the bracket is not incidental to the method, it is the method: the answer, if one exists at all, must lie strictly between zero and the ideal critical load, because the peak stress is a continuous, monotonically increasing function of the load across exactly that open interval and diverges at its upper end. A search started from an arbitrary initial guess without that bracket could step past the pole at the critical load entirely and return a spurious value from the mathematically valid but physically meaningless far side of the secant's own periodicity -- a number that would look exactly like a legitimate answer without being one.

M48   Inelastic Column Behaviour extended

Short columns yield before they buckle. The parabola is built to meet the ideal curve tangentially.

σ_cr=σ_Y[1-σ_Y (KL/r)²/(4π²E)] P=σ_c A/(1+a (L_e/r)²) P_t=π²E_t I/(KL)²

Basis

Below the critical slenderness where the ideal elastic column's predicted stress would exceed the material's own yield strength, a column no longer buckles elastically at all -- some or all of its cross-section has already yielded before the ideal critical load is reached, and its true capacity falls below the ideal elastic curve rather than following it. Several distinct, independently developed empirical and semi-empirical constructions describe that falling-away. They are built on different principles and only one of them -- the parabolic form -- is constructed to meet the ideal elastic curve tangentially at the boundary where the two regimes meet; the other two reach the same territory by blending two limiting failure modes, and by substituting a reduced stiffness into the ideal formula, respectively.

Derivation

The parabolic (Johnson) formula is constructed, not derived from first principles, by requiring a downward-curving parabola in stress against slenderness to meet the ideal Euler curve at the critical slenderness with both the same value and the same slope at that meeting point -- a tangency condition, not a coincidence, and one this module's own property checks confirm directly rather than merely assume: value and first derivative agreeing at the critical slenderness is the literal condition the parabola was built to satisfy, and a side effect of building it that way is that the parabola returns exactly half the yield stress at that same critical slenderness, a specific, checkable number rather than an approximate one. Below the critical slenderness the parabola predicts a reduced but nonzero stress, falling toward the yield stress itself as the column shortens toward zero slenderness, by construction rather than by any additional assumption.

The hyperbolic (Rankine-Gordon) form instead blends two limiting failure modes directly: a short, stocky column's actual failure by simple crushing at a stated crushing stress, and a slender column's failure by ideal elastic buckling, combined into one empirical hyperbola in slenderness that approaches the crushing load as the column shortens toward zero slenderness and approaches the ideal elastic buckling load as it lengthens, with an empirical constant fixing how quickly the transition between the two limiting behaviours occurs for a particular class of material and column.

The tangent-modulus form takes an entirely different route: rather than fitting an empirical curve to the two known limits, it uses the same ideal Euler formula unchanged in its algebraic form, but replaces the material's constant elastic modulus with the local slope of the material's own stress-strain curve at the stress level the column is actually carrying -- a modulus that itself falls once the material starts to yield, capturing the loss of stiffness directly from the material's own measured behaviour rather than through any separately fitted empirical curve.

σ_cr=σ_Y[1-σ_Y (KL/r)²/(4π²E)]
P=σ_c A/(1+a (L_e/r)²) P_t=π²E_tI/(KL)²

Assumptions

  • The parabolic form is specifically constructed to be tangent to the ideal Euler curve at the critical slenderness; using it above that slenderness, where the material has not yielded and the ideal curve alone already governs, is outside its intended range.
  • The hyperbolic form's empirical constant, and the crushing stress it is paired with, are material- and application-specific values supplied by the reader rather than derived within this module -- consistent with this app's charter, which deliberately excludes design-code-specific allowable-stress formulas.
  • The tangent-modulus form requires the local tangent modulus at the stress actually being carried, which in general depends on the load being sought -- an implicit relationship this module's closed form does not itself resolve iteratively, leaving that resolution, where needed, to the reader.

  • This app deliberately does not include the design-code-specific allowable-stress formulas (such as those found in structural steel or aluminium design specifications) that would ordinarily follow these classical constructions in a design context; the charter excludes them because they carry their own code edition and jurisdiction, which is outside this app's scope.
  • The tangent-modulus approach requires a tangent modulus as an input; this module does not itself derive that value from a stress-strain curve (the stress-strain-curve-reduction module elsewhere in this app can supply exactly that quantity, but the two are not automatically linked here).

M49   Beam-Columns extended

Axial load amplifies whatever bending is already there, without bound as the critical load is approached.

M_max=M_0 sec ((L/2)√(P/(EI))) amplification=1/(1-P/P_cr)

Basis

A beam-column is not simply a beam and a column analysed separately and added together: an axial load acting through whatever lateral deflection bending has already produced adds a further moment of its own, which increases the deflection further, which increases the moment further still -- a feedback loop that only settles into a finite equilibrium because real material stays elastic and the loop's own gain stays below one. Superposing a plain beam's bending result onto a plain column's axial result, as if the two actions were independent, misses that amplification entirely, and misses it in the unconservative direction.

Derivation

Solving the governing beam-column differential equation exactly, for a pin-ended member carrying equal end couples together with an axial load, gives the peak moment as the applied couple multiplied by a secant of an angle built from the load and the member's own bending stiffness -- the same mathematical form, and the same eventual singularity, as the eccentrically loaded column's secant relation elsewhere in this chapter, because it is the identical differential equation solved for a different boundary condition (an applied end moment here, rather than eccentricity there), and the shared singularity is proved to be the same event rather than merely observed to be: the secant's argument reaches a quarter turn, in both cases, exactly at the member's own ideal (Euler) critical load.

Alongside that exact secant result, this module carries the approximate amplification-factor form nearly every design office actually uses in practice: multiply the bending moment computed while ignoring the axial load entirely by a simple factor, one over one minus the ratio of the applied load to the critical load. The two are not offered as a choice between a "correct" and a "convenient, less-correct" method with one preferred; the whole point of carrying both is that the gap between them is itself the subject worth showing -- they agree closely, typically within a few per cent, over most of the load range an engineer would actually design into, and they diverge together, sharing the identical singularity, only as the load approaches critical. A reader who sees only the approximation has no way of knowing, from the approximation alone, how much that convenience is costing at any given load; a reader who sees both does.

An initial bow -- a member that starts out already slightly curved rather than perfectly straight, before any load is applied at all -- produces no moment on its own, since a bow with no axial load has nothing to amplify; but once axial load is applied, the load acting through that initial curvature produces a moment exactly the way an applied eccentricity does in the eccentric-column module, and the resulting additional deflection grows by the identical amplification factor. A perfectly straight member -- zero initial bow -- consequently produces zero bow-induced moment at every load right up to, but not including, the critical load itself: the idealisation the whole ideal-column module rests on, made explicit here as the special case in which this module's own bow-amplification mechanism has nothing to amplify.

M_max=M_0 sec ((L/2)√(P/(EI))) amplification=1/(1-P/P_cr)

Assumptions

  • The member is pin-ended, or treated through the same effective-length idea used throughout this chapter, and the axial load and whatever bending or initial bow it is combined with act simultaneously rather than in some sequence where one is removed before the other is applied.
  • The material remains linear-elastic at every stage of the amplification; nothing here accounts for any local yielding the amplified peak moment might actually cause.
  • The approximate amplification-factor form is understood as an approximation to the exact secant result, not a separate physical model; the two are expected to diverge as the axial load approaches the critical load, and that divergence is a property of the approximation, not a defect in either calculation.

M50   State of Stress under Combined Loading core

Axial force, two bending moments, shear and torque acting at once. The closing screen, and the whole course in one picture.

σ=N/A±M_z y/I_z±M_y z/I_y τ=VQ/(It)+Tρ/J (added as vectors, not magnitudes)

Basis

A real member rarely carries just one action: a shaft can carry an axial thrust, bending about two axes, a transverse shear force and a torque, all at the same time, and every one of those five actions contributes to the stress state at a chosen point on a chosen cross-section. This module adds nothing new to the mechanics already established in earlier chapters; it assembles their individual contributions at one point and hands the resulting combined state to the transformation and failure machinery this manual has already established -- which is why it is placed last: it is the whole subject, brought together rather than extended.

Derivation

The normal stress at a point is the direct stress from the axial force plus the bending stress each of the two moment components produces there, summed exactly the way the combined axial-and-bending module earlier in this manual already establishes for one bending direction, extended here to two. The shear stress at the same point has two physically distinct sources -- transverse shear, from whichever shear force acts on the section, and torsional shear, from any applied torque -- and combining the two correctly is the one place in this module where the mechanics is genuinely subtle rather than a straightforward sum of already-known pieces: transverse shear acts along the section in the direction of the applied shear force, while torsional shear acts circumferentially, around the section's own axis, and the angle between those two directions is not fixed. It is a full right angle only at the extreme fibre, where the transverse shear has in any case already fallen to zero, and the two directions are exactly parallel -- not perpendicular at all -- on the neutral axis, which is precisely where the transverse shear is largest. Combining the two shear stresses as though they were always perpendicular, by taking the hypotenuse of their two magnitudes, is therefore correct only at the one point where doing so costs nothing, and understates the true combined shear everywhere else, worst exactly where a shear stress check actually looks; the two must instead be resolved into components at the specific level being examined and added as the vectors they physically are. This app's own combined-loading module once did take that hypotenuse, on the strength of a comment asserting the two directions were always perpendicular -- a hypotenuse is never larger than the true vector sum, so the resulting error sat entirely in the unsafe, understating direction, and was largest at exactly the level, the neutral axis, that a shear check is most likely to examine.

Once the combined normal and shear stress at a point are known, that point is treated as a plane-stress state exactly like any other, handed directly to the same principal-stress and failure-criterion machinery established earlier in this manual -- there is no separate combined-loading version of either calculation, because a stress state assembled from five different actions is, once assembled, indistinguishable from a stress state described directly.

Finding the single worst point on the section is not answered by checking only the two obvious candidates a reader would think to try -- the extreme fibre, where bending peaks and transverse shear has vanished, and the neutral axis, where the reverse holds -- because the true worst point, where the combination of both actions is most severe, is usually somewhere between the two and depends on the section's own shape and on the relative size of every action acting at once. This module answers that by scanning the section directly rather than by solving for a stationary point analytically, for the same reason a beam's peak-moment search scans rather than solving: the combined-stress function is smooth between any width steps in the section but not across them, and a search would need to know where those steps are in any case, so scanning a candidate set built to include every point that could plausibly govern is both simpler and no less rigorous than an analytic search would be. Two of this module's own defects, both found the same way -- a candidate that should have been named explicitly but was instead left for a uniform grid to land on by chance -- illustrate exactly what "built to include every such point" has to mean in practice: on a section without symmetry about its own centroid (a tee section is the case that surfaced it), an evenly spaced grid running from one extreme fibre to the other does not, in general, land exactly on the neutral axis at all, and the scan's reported worst point came out visibly offset from where the neutral axis actually was -- with the value at that offset point correct to six figures, because the combined-stress function is nearly flat right at its own peak, and only the location wrong, at exactly the point the branching in this module asks a reader to consider explicitly. The neutral axis is consequently included in the candidate set directly, by name, rather than left to a grid to land on. A related defect in the same scan, found the same way, was probing a hair's breadth to either side of every width step without checking that the probe point had not stepped past the section's own extreme face altogether -- which evaluated cleanly to a finite, plausible-looking state a fraction of a hairsbreadth outside the material, and was every bit as wrong as a garden- variety off-by-one for being a small error rather than a large one.

τ=VQ/(It)+Tρ/J (added as vectors, not magnitudes)

Assumptions

  • The section's properties (area, second moments, first moment at the level examined) are already known from the section-properties chapter; this module assembles a stress state from them rather than deriving them afresh.
  • Torsion is combined only where the section is genuinely circular (solid or hollow), because the polar-second-moment torsion relation this module uses governs shear only for that shape; a torque on a built-up or thin-walled open or closed section is refused here by name rather than combined using a theory that does not apply to it, in favour of the dedicated torsion modules built for those shapes.

Reduce a combined action to an equivalent single action, size for both, and take the larger diameter.

Basis

A shaft carrying both a bending moment and a torque at the same station must be sized against both actions at once, and rather than checking the two separately against two different allowable stresses, the combined state at the shaft's own extreme fibre can be reduced to a single equivalent bending moment and a single equivalent torque, each of which, sized in the ordinary single-action way, gives a diameter that keeps the actual combined stress within whichever of the two allowable stresses -- normal or shear -- would otherwise be exceeded first.

Derivation

At the extreme fibre of a circular shaft under combined bending and torsion, the normal stress from bending and the shear stress from torsion together define a plane-stress state whose maximum principal stress and maximum shear stress can each be written out directly in terms of the applied moment and torque; recognising that those two expressions have exactly the same algebraic form as the moment a pure bending problem, or the torque a pure torsion problem, would need to produce that same peak stress gives the equivalent moment and equivalent torque directly, each built from the same combination of the applied moment and torque under a square root. M52   Riveted and Bolted Joints extended

Three ways to fail, and the joint is only as strong as the weakest. Naming the winner is the whole point.

P_s=n(π d²/4)τ P_b=n d t σ_b P_t=(p-d) t σ_t η= min(P_s,P_b,P_t)/(p t σ_t)

Basis

A riveted or bolted lap joint can fail three distinct ways -- the fasteners themselves can be sheared through, the plate can crush locally against the fasteners (bearing failure), or the plate can tear across the net section remaining between the holes -- and the joint as a whole carries no more load than whichever of the three failure loads is smallest. The number a designer actually needs is not three separate strengths but a single stated conclusion: which one of the three governs, because that is the specific detail a design change would have to address to make the joint stronger.

Derivation

Each of the three failure loads follows from a direct strength calculation for its own distinct failure mechanism: rivet shear is the total cross-sectional area of every fastener, on however many shear planes each one is cut on, multiplied by the material's allowable shear stress; bearing is the total projected contact area between the fasteners and the plate (each fastener's diameter times the plate thickness) multiplied by the allowable bearing stress; and tearing is the net width of plate actually remaining between adjacent holes -- the pitch less one hole diameter -- times the plate thickness and the allowable tensile stress, which is directly why the tearing strength falls to exactly zero as the pitch closes in on the hole diameter itself: at that limit there is, quite literally, no solid plate left between adjacent holes to carry any tension at all. The joint's overall strength is the smallest of the three, and its efficiency -- how much of the plate's own unbroken strength the joint actually achieves -- is that governing strength divided by what the same width of plate would carry with no holes in it at all, a ratio that can never exceed one by construction, since a joint made from a plate with holes cut into it cannot be stronger than the same plate before any holes were made.

Reducing three numbers to a single named conclusion is not a display convenience layered on top of the calculation; it is treated as part of the calculation's own output, because reading the wrong one of the three numbers as though it were the governing one over-predicts the joint's true strength -- and the everyday intuition that "the strength is roughly the smallest of these three" still leaves a reader to identify which is smallest and to reason about what changing it would do, which is precisely the step this module's own governing-mode output performs directly rather than leaving to inspection.

P_s=n(π d²/4)τ P_b=n d t σ_b
P_t=(p-d) t σ_t η= min(P_s,P_b,P_t)/(p t σ_t)

Assumptions

  • All fasteners in the joint are identical in diameter and share the load equally; this module does not itself distribute an unequal load among a group of dissimilar fasteners.
  • The three failure modes are evaluated independently, each against its own allowable stress already reflecting whatever safety factor is appropriate to that specific mode; this module does not itself derive an allowable stress from a fastener grade or a design code.
  • The tearing calculation is for a single line of holes across the plate's width; a staggered (zig-zag) pattern of holes changes the net section available to tear across and needs that geometry accounted for separately.

  • Doubling the shear planes doubles the shear strength exactly

Where it stops.

  • Two modes give equal strength — Crossover

Solution method

Direct evaluation, entirely closed form, for all three failure loads and the resulting governing strength and efficiency; nothing here iterates. The governing mode is determined by a direct three-way comparison, named explicitly as a conclusion rather than left as three numbers for a reader to compare unaided. A rule-of-thumb starting diameter for a rivet, given only the plate thickness, is also provided as a conventional first estimate, distinct from -- and not a substitute for -- the three strength checks the module actually verifies a chosen diameter against.

Limitations

  • This module covers the three classical failure modes of a simple lap or butt joint under a straightforward tension load; it does not evaluate eccentric loading on a group of fasteners, which is instead the province of the welded-joint module's own eccentric-loading treatment, extended by analogy rather than reused directly.
  • It assumes a regular, single-line pattern of identical fasteners; block shear (a combined shear-and-tension tear-out path around a group of fasteners near a plate edge) is a distinct, real failure mode this module does not check.
  • The rule-of-thumb rivet diameter is a conventional starting estimate only, not a substitute for verifying the resulting design against all three failure strengths this module computes.

Reading the result

The single conclusion this screen is built to deliver -- which of the three modes governs, and by how much the next-weakest mode exceeds it -- is more useful for design decisions than any of the three raw numbers alone, because it says directly what a change to the joint would need to target: a shear-governed joint gets stronger from a larger or an extra fastener, a bearing-governed one from a thicker plate or a larger fastener diameter, and a tearing-governed one from a wider pitch, and improving the wrong one of the three leaves the joint's true strength exactly where it was.

M53   Welded Joints extended

A fillet weld fails across its throat. Eccentric loading adds a torsional component that must be summed as a vector.

t_throat=0.707s P=0.707 s L_w τ_allow τ_res=|τ⃗_direct+τ⃗_torsional|

Basis

A fillet weld does not fail across the leg dimension a welder actually lays down; it fails across its throat, the shortest path through the weld metal connecting the two members, which for an equal-legged fillet is smaller than the leg by a fixed geometric factor. A weld loaded through the centroid of its own group carries only that direct shear across the throat, but a load applied off-centre from the weld group's own centroid additionally twists the group, adding a torsional shear component that must be combined with the direct one as the vector quantities they physically are, not simply added as magnitudes.

Derivation

The throat of an equal-legged fillet weld is the perpendicular distance from the weld's own root to the hypotenuse of the right-angled triangle its cross-section forms, which for two equal legs is the leg length divided by the square root of two -- the same geometric factor, inverted, that appears throughout this app wherever a forty-five-degree diagonal of a right isosceles triangle is involved. The strength of a run of fillet weld follows directly as that throat dimension times the weld's own effective length and the material's allowable shear stress.

Where a load is applied with some eccentricity from a weld group's own centroid, that eccentricity is statically equivalent to the same load acting through the centroid together with a twisting moment equal to the load times the eccentricity -- exactly the same equivalent-force-and-couple reasoning the combined-loading and eccentric-column modules elsewhere in this app use for an off-centre axial load. The direct shear this produces is uniform across the whole weld group, while the torsional shear this twisting moment produces at any point in the group varies with that point's own distance from the centroid and acts perpendicular to the line from the centroid to that point -- which means the two are perpendicular to each other only at specific points around the group, and in general must be resolved into components and added as vectors rather than combined by adding their magnitudes, which would overstate the true peak stress, sometimes by a wide margin, exactly the same class of error this app's own combined-loading module was once found to make in the opposite (unsafe) direction for a different pair of shear stresses. The polar moment governing how strongly the weld group resists twisting is the sum of the group's own second moments of area about two perpendicular axes through its centroid -- an ordinary polar second moment, following the identical perpendicular-axis relation the section-properties chapter establishes for any planar shape, applied here to the weld group's own layout rather than to a solid cross-section.

t_throat=0.707s P=0.707 s L_w τ_allow
τ_res=|τ⃗_direct+τ⃗_torsional|

Assumptions

  • Every weld run in the group is an equal-legged fillet weld, so the fixed throat factor applies uniformly across the whole group.
  • The weld group's polar moment and centroid are computed from the group's own two-dimensional layout, treating each weld run as a line of material rather than modelling the full three-dimensional weld cross-section.
  • Direct and torsional shear are evaluated at the specific point in the group being checked -- ordinarily the point farthest from the group's own centroid, where the torsional contribution is largest -- rather than as a single value assumed to apply uniformly across the whole group.

What must always be true here. Each of these is a test in the suite, not a remark:

  • Zero eccentricity reduces the result to direct shear alone
  • The weld-group polar moment obeys the same parallel-axis rule as any section
  • Peak weld stress grows linearly with eccentricity once the group has any extent

Where it stops.

  • Weld group of zero extent — The polar moment vanishes and the torsional term diverges

Solution method

Direct evaluation, entirely closed form. The direct and torsional shear components are resolved along a common pair of directions and combined by summing components before taking the resultant magnitude, never by adding the two magnitudes directly, for the same physical reason -- and following the same discipline -- as the combined-loading module's own transverse-and- torsional shear combination earlier in this manual.

Limitations

  • This module covers equal-legged fillet welds under direct and eccentric in-plane shear; groove welds, unequal-leg fillets, and out-of-plane (bending-inducing) eccentricity on a weld group are all outside its closed forms.
  • The weld group's polar moment is computed from its own planar layout as a set of lines; it does not represent the actual three-dimensional stress distribution through a real weld's own cross-section, which is a simplification standard to this style of analysis rather than a peculiarity of this module.
  • As with the riveted-joint module beside it, fatigue under a cyclically varying load is outside this module's static strength calculation.

Reading the result

A weld group loaded exactly through its own centroid is the useful sanity check for this module, because the eccentricity term then vanishes identically and the result must reduce to plain, uniform direct shear across the throat -- any nonzero torsional contribution appearing at zero eccentricity signals an error in how the load's point of application was described, not a legitimate result. Where the eccentricity is genuine, the peak stress is not generally at the point closest to the load; it is at whichever point in the group has the largest combined direct-plus- torsional vector, which is usually, but not automatically, the point farthest from the group's own centroid.
