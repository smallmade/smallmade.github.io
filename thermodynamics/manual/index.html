Usage Manual

How to use the screens that exist today. All 29 modules planned for this release are built; this page documents each one against the app itself, not against a plan for it.

What's covered: all 29 v1.0 screens, described below in the same groups the app's own sidebar uses. Every screen has been opened on its own default inputs and checked against an independently run Python reference value. Depth varies with how much a screen actually does — a five-field lookup gets a paragraph, a nine-station cycle with a live diagram gets a full section — but every entry says what the screen computes, what you type in, and, where a screen deliberately implements less than its module's full canon (several do — a second cycle root, a control-volume entropy balance, multistage compression and more are each a real, named gap rather than an oversight), what's missing and why. Each section links to its formula, full symbol table and derivation context in the theory manual, which covers the physics regardless of whether a screen exists for it; this manual covers the screens.

One pattern is common to all 29 and worth knowing before the screens themselves: every one opens with a small, monospaced formula card showing its headline relation, transcribed by hand into plain notation — fractions written inline, Greek letters as ordinary characters, rates marked with a dot over the symbol. SwiftUI has no built-in renderer for the typeset version the theory manual shows, so this plain-text card is what the app itself displays. It's a reminder of the relation in play, not a substitute for the derivation — for that, follow the theory-manual link at the end of each section below.

State & Properties

State Explorer

Two independent properties fix the state of a simple compressible substance. This screen accepts an admissible pair of properties for water/steam and reports the rest, with the phase region named rather than implied.

Choosing what you know

A picker at the top offers five pairs of properties. Pick the pair you have; the two matching input fields are open for typing and every other field on the screen shows, in place of a value, the reason it isn't open — never just a greyed-out blank.

PairThe other three fields show
pressure and temperaturequality — not a two-phase state, quality does not apply; enthalpy and entropy — computed from pressure and temperature
pressure and qualitytemperature — fixed by the saturation line at this pressure; enthalpy and entropy — computed from the quality
temperature and qualitypressure — fixed by the saturation line at this temperature; enthalpy and entropy — computed from the quality
pressure and enthalpytemperature — solved for from pressure and enthalpy; quality — solved for, if the state is a mixture; entropy — computed from the state
pressure and entropytemperature — solved for from pressure and entropy; quality — solved for, if the state is a mixture; enthalpy — computed from the state

The canon behind this screen lists nine state-fixing pairs in total (it also allows fixing from temperature-and-specific-volume, pressure-and-specific-volume, enthalpy-and-entropy, or internal-energy-and- specific-volume). Only the five above are wired up today; the rest are a later pass, not a decision that they don't matter.

The five properties

The two open input fields don't print a unit next to them while you type — type the same units shown in the table above and in the output rows (temperature in kelvin, pressure in MPa). Water is validated from about 0.0006 MPa (the triple point) to 100 MPa, and from 273.15 K up to 2273.15 K.

What comes back

A fixed state reports temperature, pressure, specific volume, density, internal energy, enthalpy and entropy, each printed beside the relation that produced it (in the app's own words, not retypeset — e.g. next to enthalpy you'll see the plain line h = u + pv). The number of digits shown is fixed per quantity, not uniform: temperature to 2 decimal places, pressure to 4 significant figures, specific volume to 5, enthalpy to 2 decimals, entropy to 4.

Quality is the one exception: it only appears when the state is strictly inside the two-phase dome (between 0 and 1). A superheated or compressed state doesn't print x = 1.0000 or x = 0.0000 — both would teach the wrong idea, so the row is left off entirely rather than shown as a number that doesn't mean anything there.

Above the results, the screen names the phase region rather than leaving you to infer it from the numbers: compressed liquid, saturated liquid, saturated mixture, saturated vapour, superheated vapour, or supercritical.

Notes the screen adds on its own

Always shown

Values come from the current international formulation. Older printed tables were computed from an earlier one and differ in the fourth figure, most visibly in the saturated-vapour columns. Neither is a mistake; they are different formulations, and this one is the one still maintained.

Compressed liquid states

The saturated-liquid approximation is being used. Its error grows with pressure; the pressure-corrected form and the exact value are both available.

Near the critical point

Within about 0.6 K of the critical point the latent quantities vanish and quality loses meaning. Values here carry an absolute error floor, not a relative one.

When a pair doesn't work

Two situations end without a result, and both say why in place of a number rather than showing a wrong one or crashing:

  • The pair is outside the water formulation's validated range, or lands somewhere the solver doesn't converge.
  • Pressure and temperature are given together on the saturation line itself — there, every quality from 0 to 1 shares the same pressure and temperature, so the pair doesn't fix a state at all. The screen says this explicitly and suggests giving quality, enthalpy, or entropy instead.

One thing this screen doesn't do yet: where a pair genuinely admits two different states (some enthalpy-and-entropy inputs land near the dome twice), the canon calls for showing both as a visible choice. Today only one root comes back. Not a silent wrong answer, but not the full behaviour either.

M02 · Ideal Gas Properties

Temperature alone fixes every caloric property of an ideal gas. Pick one of eight pure species and a temperature, and the screen reads off its molar mass, gas constant, cp, cv, specific heat ratio k, enthalpy, internal energy, the standard-state entropy function, and the relative pressure and relative volume used for isentropic shortcuts — all from a seven-term polynomial fit in temperature. A second, independent panel on the same screen takes a start and end temperature and pressure and reports the entropy change between them.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
substancesubstance—Which of eight species (N₂, O₂, H₂O, CO₂, CO, H₂, CH₄, Ar) the polynomial is evaluated for.
temperatureTKTemperature: the property two bodies share when nothing flows between them on contact.
temperature, in / outTKThe two temperatures of the entropy-change panel.
pressure, in / outpMPaThe two pressures of the entropy-change panel.

What comes back: molar mass M (kg/kmol), gas constant R (kJ/(kg K)), cp and cv (kJ/(kg K)), k, enthalpy h and internal energy u (kJ/kg), the standard-state entropy function s° (kJ/(kg K)), and the relative pressure pr and relative volume vr used for isentropic process shortcuts elsewhere in the app — then, from the second panel, the entropy change ΔS between the two given states.

The canon lists three specific-heat models for this module: variable cp read straight from the polynomial, a constant cp evaluated once at a stated temperature, and a cold-air-standard constant k. The first two are really the same calculation used two ways — holding cp constant just means reading it once at one temperature and using that number by hand, not a separate mode switch. The third has no backing constant anywhere in the underlying engine yet; the same gap recurs in the four piston-cycle screens and the Brayton screens further down, and is noted again there. The substance picker also offers only pure species, not the humid-air mixture the app's psychrometric engine separately knows about: the mixture functions cover cp, h, s° and k but not cv or a mixture gas constant, so this screen doesn't guess at cv = cp − R for a mixture without a stated mixture R.

M34 · Real Gas: Compressibility and Cubic Equations of State

At one pressure and temperature, this screen puts six equations of state side by side — the ideal gas law, the generalised compressibility relation (evaluated from its own equation rather than read off a digitised chart), and four cubic equations of state (van der Waals, Redlich-Kwong, Soave-Redlich-Kwong, Peng-Robinson) — and measures each one's molar volume and compressibility factor Z against the IAPWS-IF97 reference formulation for water. It's an accuracy demonstration as much as a calculator: the point is seeing where each model stops being usable, not picking one to trust.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
pressurepMPaPressure: the normal force a fluid exerts per unit of the area it pushes on.
temperatureTKTemperature: the property two bodies share when nothing flows between them on contact.

There's no substance picker, and that's deliberate rather than a missing field: this screen always compares against IAPWS-IF97 water as the fixed, exact reference, because showing how far a cubic equation of state drifts from the real formulation is the whole point — modelling an arbitrary gas is a different screen's job. What comes back is a reference block (molar volume, compressibility Z, reduced temperature Tr, reduced pressure Pr) and then a table with one row per model — volume, Z, and percent error against the reference, the error highlighted when it passes 5%. The shipped default sits where the models visibly disagree (the ideal gas law is off by about 38%, the better cubics by under 2%) rather than at a point where every model reads the same and the comparison teaches nothing.

The canon also describes a visible choice between the two physical roots a cubic equation can return in the two-phase region (the larger root is vapour, the smaller is liquid, and the middle root is mechanically unstable and never returned). This screen doesn't show a control for choosing between them; whichever root the comparison settles on is what's shown.

First Law

M03 · Process Path

A quasi-equilibrium path between two states — isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, isentropic, or polytropic — with the boundary work computed from the path itself, W = ∫ p dV. Isentropic and polytropic share one calculation: leaving the polytropic exponent blank fits it from the two given states either way, so there's no separate isentropic-only code path to reason about.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning

specific volume, in / outvm³/kgSpecific volume at each end of the path.
exponent (polytropic only)n—Polytropic exponent: the constant in pvⁿ = const. Left blank, it's fitted from the two states instead of typed in.

enthalpy, in / outhkJ/kgGiven directly — see below.

What comes back: boundary work w (kJ/kg) and its total W (kJ); then, from the first law, ΔU, heat q and its total Q, ΔH and ΔS. Every specific quantity has its own total row rather than folding mass into it silently — the same lesson Closed System Energy Balance's residual is there to catch applies here too.

This screen does not derive ΔU, ΔH or ΔS from the path or from State Explorer automatically. The underlying calculation only knows pressure and volume; it doesn't know the substance and can't compute a property change on its own. So the internal energy, enthalpy and entropy at each end are typed in directly rather than looked up — matching what the canon's own entry list actually declares as given, but worth knowing before you go looking for a substance picker that isn't there.

M04 · Closed System Energy Balance

The first law for a closed system, ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE = Q − W, solved for whichever of heat, work or the internal energy change is unknown. Kinetic and potential energy terms are included (inlet/exit velocity and elevation, defaulting to zero) so a problem that actually uses them doesn't need a second screen.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
solve for——Which of Q, W or ΔU the screen computes; the other two are given.
massmkgMass of the system.
heatQkJHeat transferred across the boundary, positive into the system. Given unless it's the unknown.
workWkJWork transferred across the boundary, positive out of the system. Given unless it's the unknown.
internal energy changeΔUkJGiven unless it's the unknown.
velocity, in / outvelm/sDefaults to zero; only matters when it's fast enough to compete with the other terms.

What comes back: Q, W, ΔU, ΔKE, ΔPE (all kJ), and a residual — how far from zero the balance still sits at the solved answer, reported rather than assumed, so convergence is something you can see rather than take on faith.

The canon lists a fourth quantity you might solve for — mass — but the underlying calculation refuses it outright and points back to State Explorer instead: solving for mass needs a full state, not just a balance, so the picker here only ever offers the three that actually resolve. The canon also lists six work modes that can act at once (moving boundary, shaft, electrical, spring, surface tension, a generalised force and displacement) and notes they add together; this screen takes W as a single already-summed number rather than breaking it down by mode, so combining several modes is arithmetic you do before typing the total in.

M05 · Cycle Energy Accounting

The bookkeeping every cycle shares, reduced to its simplest form: given heat in and heat out over one complete cycle, net work is their difference, and three ratios of that same arithmetic — thermal efficiency, a refrigeration coefficient of performance, a heat-pump coefficient of performance — are shown together rather than behind a device-kind picker. They're three readings of one energy balance, not three separate calculations, and Reversible Limits further down makes the same choice for the same reason.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
heat inQinkJTotal heat supplied to the cycle over one complete circuit.
heat outQoutkJTotal heat rejected by the cycle over one complete circuit.

What comes back: net work Wnet (kJ), thermal efficiency η, refrigeration COP β, heat-pump COP γ. Both heat quantities have to be zero or positive, or the screen says so instead of returning a number.

This module has no screen-specific engine of its own and no other screen builds on it — it's the generic version of the bookkeeping underneath the nine cycle-topology screens further down (Rankine and its variants, the piston cycles, Brayton and its variants), not a front end for any one of them.

M06 · Nozzle and Diffuser

Two devices, one balance: choose nozzle (velocity rises, pressure falls) or diffuser (velocity falls, pressure rises) and the screen solves the same steady-flow energy balance either way. This is the one control-volume device in the app where the kinetic-energy term is the point rather than a quantity everyone else safely drops.

A note (not an error) appears when the exit comes out transonic or lands inside the vapour dome; the shipped defaults stay comfortably subsonic so the screen opens clean, and reaching the sonic case is one exit-pressure edit away.

M10 · Throttling Device

A constant-enthalpy expansion across a restriction: given the inlet state and the exit pressure, the screen holds enthalpy fixed to find the exit state, and reports the entropy this necessarily irreversible process produces — a throttle is adiabatic by construction, so the entropy balance behind this number has no boundary heat-transfer term to enter.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
inlet pressurepMPaPressure entering the throttle.
inlet temperatureTKTemperature entering the throttle.
exit pressurepMPaPressure leaving the throttle, lower than the inlet.

What comes back: the inlet state (labelled as the enthalpy held constant across the valve), the exit state, and the entropy production σ (kJ/(kg K)).

The canon lists three uses for this module: finding the downstream state, a throttling calorimeter (inferring an unmeasurable upstream quality from a measurable downstream pressure and temperature after throttling), and mapping the Joule-Thomson inversion locus. Only the first is built. Neither the calorimeter direction nor the Joule-Thomson coefficient itself — whose sign decides whether throttling cools or warms a given gas — is on this screen; the coefficient needs a volume-expansivity derivative the screen doesn't otherwise compute.

M11 · Transient Filling and Emptying

Charging a vessel from a supply line, or discharging one to a lower pressure, tracking how its contents evolve. A direction toggle switches between two genuinely different calculations: charging solves one energy balance in a single step, while discharging integrates the vessel's state through many intermediate steps as it empties, because the properties of the stream leaving keep changing as the vessel's own state changes.

Near water's triple point

The discharge defaults (water, 0.5 to 0.2 MPa starting at 373.15 K) were chosen to stay clear of a real numerical edge found while building this screen, not picked arbitrarily. An earlier, equally plausible set of starting numbers drove the vessel's temperature, over the course of the several-hundred-step integration discharge uses internally, to within a few thousandths of a kelvin of water's own solver floor near 274 K. Both languages the app is built from agree on the physically correct answer right at that edge — but that close to it, two independently written bounded root-finders can take different floating-point paths to the same nominal algorithm, and one can fail to bracket a root where the other still finds one. It isn't a wrong answer; it's a domain edge neither solver is obliged to handle identically to the other. Numbers that push a discharge close to 274 K are worth double-checking rather than trusting on the first try.

Second Law

M12 · Reversible Limits

The bound every other cycle screen is measured against. Given a hot and a cold reservoir temperature, this screen always shows all three reversible (Carnot) bounds together — heat-engine efficiency, refrigeration COP, heat-pump COP — rather than behind a device-kind picker, since they're three readings of the same two temperatures rather than three separate calculations (Cycle Energy Accounting above makes the same choice for the same reason). Given an actual cycle's heat in and heat out as well, it adds a verdict.

M14 · Isentropic Efficiency

The reverse direction from Turbine and Compressor and Pump above: instead of an efficiency you already know, this screen takes an inlet state and an actual exit state — one you measured, or one a problem gives you — and works out the isentropic efficiency they imply, for a turbine, a compressor, or a pump.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
devicedevice—Turbine or pump (water) or compressor (gas).
inlet pressure / temperaturep, TMPa, KState entering the device.
exit pressurepMPaPressure leaving the device.
actual exit temperatureTKThe measured or given exit temperature — this, not an efficiency, is what you supply.
substance (compressor only)substance—One of eight gas species.

What comes back: for the turbine and pump, inlet, actual-exit and isentropic-exit state blocks plus isentropic work, actual work and the resulting isentropic efficiency; for the compressor, the same three figures without the state blocks (gas states are shown as a plainer T/p/h readout elsewhere in the app, not this screen's compact water-state block).

Sign convention

Turbine work and pump or compressor work are opposite in sign by convention. A turbine's ideal and actual work are each (inlet enthalpy minus exit enthalpy), positive because enthalpy drops as the device does work; a pump's or compressor's are each (exit enthalpy minus inlet enthalpy), positive because enthalpy rises as work is done on the fluid. The screen applies whichever is correct for the selected device automatically. If the actual exit state you type in would call for negative work from a turbine — enthalpy rising instead of falling — the efficiency is shown as -- rather than a number, because a work-producing device can't have a negative isentropic efficiency.

The canon's fourth device, the nozzle, is left out: a nozzle's efficiency is defined on a ratio of kinetic energies, not the enthalpy-drop work ratio the other three devices share, so it doesn't fit this screen's shared work-in/work-out shape without a second, differently structured set of inputs.

M16 · Closed-System Exergy

How much work the contents of a system could still do against a stated environment. Rather than typing eight raw property values (internal energy, specific volume and entropy, for both the system and the dead state), this screen takes pressure and temperature for each and derives the rest the same way State Explorer fixes a state.

Two independent calculators on one screen. The second is a generic exergetic efficiency — product exergy divided by supplied exergy — from two numbers you type in directly.

Power Cycles

Eight screens below share one shape: a form of cycle-specific inputs at the top, and, below a divider, the same results panel underneath. That panel is worth describing once rather than eight times:

  • A segmented control choosing which pair of properties to plot the cycle against — temperature-entropy (the default, the plane the second law is visible as geometry on), pressure-specific-volume, pressure-enthalpy, or enthalpy-entropy. The two pressure-based planes use a logarithmic pressure axis, because pressure spans about four decades across a real power cycle and a linear axis would crush the condenser end of the diagram against zero.
  • A live chart below the picker: the cycle's own state points, connected leg by leg, with the two-phase dome drawn in behind them as a dashed reference curve. It redraws immediately when any input changes.
  • A station table, one row per state point: phase, temperature, pressure, enthalpy, entropy, quality.
  • A performance section, each figure shown beside the relation that produced it — the same "never a number alone" rule State Explorer's result rows follow.
  • A "worth knowing" notes section when the underlying calculation has something to say.

If a cycle doesn't resolve for the given inputs, the panel shows why in place of a chart. Only the input form below is specific to each of the eight screens that follow; none of them need this panel re-explained.

M21 · Vapour Plant Exergy Audit

Component-by-component exergy destruction for a Rankine cycle, built right on this screen from a compact form, given a heat-source temperature and a dead-state environment. Where the energy balance can only ever say where the energy went, this answers where the ability to do work was lost, ranked from the biggest loss down.

FieldSymbolUnitMeaning
boiler / condenser pressurepMPaSame cycle inputs as Rankine Cycle above — this screen builds its own cycle rather than reading one from elsewhere.
turbine inlet temperatureTKSuperheat entering the turbine.
turbine / pump efficiencyη_t, η_p—Isentropic efficiencies.
source temperatureTsourceKTemperature at which heat enters the plant — fixes how much exergy came in with it.
dead-state temperature / pressureT0, p0K, MPaThe reference environment everything is measured against.

What comes back: a horizontal bar showing at a glance how the incoming exergy splits between net work delivered and exergy destroyed by each cause; a summary (exergy supplied, net work, exergetic efficiency, and whether the breakdown actually closes); and a per-component table, one row per device, each with the exergy it destroys and its share of the total. Components destroying less than half a percent of the total are folded into one "smaller components" line rather than cluttering the ranking with noise. The screen also calls out in words whichever component destroys the most — typically the heat transfer into the working fluid, before any work has been produced, for a plant like this one.

Where the boundary is drawn

The audit draws its system boundary either at the environment itself, in which case the whole temperature drop across heat rejection counts as destroyed exergy and nothing is carried out unaccounted for, or at a warmer sink temperature, in which case part of the rejected heat's exergy is carried out of the plant rather than destroyed inside it. The screen states which case applies as one of its notes, since the two read very differently.

If the numbers don't add up

The screen checks its own arithmetic: if net work plus everything destroyed and carried out doesn't sum back to the exergy supplied within a tight tolerance, it says so explicitly and calls the breakdown unreliable until that's resolved, rather than presenting a ranking that quietly doesn't balance.

The underlying audit only ever works on water/Rankine cycles — it cannot be pointed at a Brayton, Otto, Diesel or any other gas cycle built elsewhere in the app, which is why this screen builds its own compact Rankine form rather than offering a picker over cycles built on other screens. The exergy supplied is specified only as heat crossing the boundary at a stated source temperature; the canon's other listed option, accounting for fuel chemical exergy once combustion is modelled, doesn't exist yet, so a real plant's combustion irreversibility — typically the largest single loss of all — sits outside this audit's boundary. The boundary note the screen prints says as much rather than omitting it silently.

What comes back: heating COP γ, cooling COP β, a reversible heating COP for comparison, heating capacity (kW), compressor power (kW), heat absorbed (kW), mass flow, suction volume, the evaporator temperature the machine actually runs at, and a yes/no frosting-risk flag.

Why displacement, not mass flow

The machine is sized by a fixed compressor displacement rather than a fixed mass flow rate, and that choice matters for reading the numbers correctly. As the outdoor temperature falls, the suction gas gets less dense, so the mass flow a fixed-displacement compressor can actually move falls too — on top of, and independent from, the COP already falling. A model that assumed constant mass flow instead would systematically overstate how much heat the machine can still deliver in cold weather.

Same refrigerant caveat as the refrigeration screen: CO₂ is the substance the shipped defaults are guaranteed to resolve, and the modest default indoor temperature is deliberate too — CO₂'s critical point is only about 31°C, and a warmer indoor default combined with the condenser approach would push the condensing side past it. A related but different question — the outdoor balance-point temperature below which this machine's capacity can no longer meet a building's heat loss — is explicitly not this screen's job; the calculation behind it exists, but plotting it against a load line is left for a possible separate screen, matching the app's own one-screen-one-thing rule.
